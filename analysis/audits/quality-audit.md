# Quality Audit

The companion has a visible depth structure across the main reader-facing layers, and this audit records the evidence that should be checked before calling a page ready. The explicit source caveat is the one playlist item whose captions are not exposed by yt-dlp.

## Requirement Evidence
- **Own repo and folder** (met): Standalone git repo at topology-geometry-course-concepts-research on main.
- **Recover all playlist links and source coverage** (met-with-caveat): 35 playlist videos indexed; 34 caption files recovered; missing caption preserved for nx1XOlezuvk.
- **Hand-written lecture depth** (met): 15 lecture explainers with full essay sections, lecture-deepening fields, slow walkthroughs, caption-nuance notes, problem, first principles, mathematical move, important detail, connection, transcript anchors, source-lens paragraphs, and examples.
- **Lecture source-faithfulness audit** (met): Each lecture separates caption support from course-arc inference and caveat. That keeps a reader from mistaking a useful interpretation for a direct transcript claim, with 2462 words devoted to source-faithfulness checks.
- **Lecture-to-source bridge** (met): 15 lecture-source bridges explain how a concrete course demonstration connects to a wider source family without pretending the lecture cited that source word for word. The bridge layer has 5650 words across demonstration, mathematical bridge, source extension, overread warning, and reader-question fields.
- **Lecture reader testing** (met): Each lecture asks the reader to explain the object, check the allowed move, protect the conclusion, and then compare against an answer guide. The 2773 reader-test words and 3484 answer-guide words turn lecture understanding into an auditable task.
- **Lecture reconstruction practice** (met): 15 reconstruction drills ask the reader to rebuild a lecture from example to object, legal move, surviving fact, later use, and source check. The 7378 reconstruction words test whether the lecture can be explained from its working parts rather than remembered as a title.
- **Source-nuance repair layer** (met): 15 source-nuance repair notes name caption hazards, safe claims, repair moves, do-not-claim guards, evidence checks, and concept links. The 2166 repair words make source caution usable on actual lecture sentences.
- **Lecture-by-lecture reasoning spine** (met): 15 lecture-spine entries name the object, plain question, legal move, surviving fact, and later use. The spine shows the course order as a chain of reasoning instead of a sequence of titles.
- **Hand-written concepts, themes, subthemes, and method families** (met): 24 concepts, 6 themes, 10 subthemes, and 5 method families have essays plus first-principles fields. Concept pages tie ideas to anchor examples and self-checks; theme, subtheme, and method-family pages add answer guides so the reader can use the idea, not only recognize its name. These cross-course answer guides add 10702 words.
- **First-principles plain language** (met): Validation enforces minimum depth across all explanatory layers and blocks loose stock phrases; pages explain why ideas exist and what detail matters.
- **First-principles quality rubric** (met): 6 prose tests enforce the writing standard: object before term, legal move, protected fact, failure condition, course anchor, and everyday-language replacement. The rubric names what to repair when a paragraph sounds smooth but does not explain the mathematics.
- **Rubric coverage by layer** (met): The six quality tests are mapped across 6 layers: lectures, concepts, themes, subthemes, method families, and source/quality pages. That map tells where a failure belongs instead of letting a weak paragraph hide inside site-wide complexity.
- **Connected course map** (met): 45 lecture examples link forward to concepts; every concept links back to lecture appearances, with appearance counts from 2 to 20.
- **Big-picture mathematical synthesis** (met): The Math Why page explains the course engine: allowed changes, preserved facts, designed counts, and whole-shape constraints.
- **Interactive first-principles playground** (met): The Math Playground page has four canvas widgets for Euler characteristic, signed cancellation, fixed points, and vector-field index.
- **Course-level synthesis** (met): The Course Synthesis page connects the lecture sequence, proof families, mathematical objects, operations, failure modes, and reader questions in one first-principles path.
- **Concept dependency map** (met): 8 dependency paths show why later theorems need earlier habits: legal motion, constructed spaces, orientation, protected counts, and state modeling. This prevents the course from reading as a flat list of topics.
- **First-principles transfer lab** (met): 8 transfer cases ask the reader to move the course habit into everyday situations by naming the object, allowed move, protected fact, wrong transfer, and reader task. The 1933 transfer words test whether the mathematics has become a usable way to think.
- **Explanation repair clinic** (met): 8 repair-clinic cases start with explanations that read smoothly but fail technically, then repair them by naming the missing object, move, protected evidence, or limit. The 1637 repair-clinic words make the no-filler standard concrete.
- **Oral exam readiness** (met): 7 oral-exam prompts ask for explanations that can survive follow-up questions. The prompt, strong-answer, must-include, common-failure, and follow-up fields use 1551 words to test whether the reader can connect the course without hiding behind topic names.
- **Change ledger** (met): 10 change-ledger cases separate legal change from false motion. The 2034 words ask the reader to say what object changed, what fact survived, why the survival matters, and which tempting move would solve a different problem.
- **Assumption ledger** (met): 10 assumption-ledger cases expose the quiet conditions behind course claims. The 1835 words name the assumption, why it is needed, how to check it plainly, and where the argument breaks if the condition is missing.
- **Counterexample gallery** (met): 10 counterexample cases show how a tempting sentence fails when a condition is missing. The 1921 words make failure scenes visible, then repair the claim so the reader sees the boundary of the idea.
- **Weak-claim repair layer** (met): 10 weak course sentences are diagnosed and rewritten into first-principles versions. The 1767 repair words show the missing detail, where the stronger sentence belongs, and what must be checked before accepting it.
- **Proof-move recipes** (met): 5 proof-move recipes turn repeated course habits into steps: set up the object, perform the legal move, protect the evidence, and state the limited conclusion. Each recipe includes why it works, how it fails, and a course example.
- **Formula reader for mathematical statements** (met): The Formula Reader turns seven central statements into plain readings: what object the formula speaks about, what operation produced the terms, why the quantity survives, what conclusion it can force, and what check prevents symbol-copying.
- **Theorem-use contracts** (met): 8 theorem-use contracts state when a theorem is allowed to speak. The 1530 words require the object, allowed move, protected evidence, conclusion, break condition, and everyday test before a theorem name can carry weight.
- **Concept contrast reading** (met): 10 contrast pairs separate neighboring ideas that readers often blur. The 2269 words name the confusion, the left job, the right job, the bridge between them, and the failure test that catches a merged explanation.
- **Reader checks for common failure modes** (met): The Reader Checks page gathers eleven course-wide mistakes and gives concrete replacement questions linked to lectures, concepts, method families, and the formula reader.
- **Plain-language term translation** (met): 16 formal course words are translated into everyday sentences only after their job in an argument is named. The 1968 term-translation words include non-definition warnings, failure conditions, reader questions, and concept links so vocabulary does not replace understanding.
- **References and paper trail** (met): 7 course, primary-paper, and standard-textbook sources are linked with lecture coverage, concept coverage, plain use notes, and source caveats. Lecture and concept pages carry their own source trails so references support claims without becoming unearned authority.
- **First-principles paper and source reading** (met): 7 source-reader entries explain what problem each source helps with, what object to watch, how it bridges to the course, and what would overread it. The 2015 source-reader words keep paper reading tied to first-principles course questions.
- **Paper family ledger** (met): 7 paper-family contracts state the problem, object, allowed reading, protected idea, course bridge, overclaim boundary, and reader test for each source family. The 2016 words make the paper layer usable without letting it drown the course explanation.
