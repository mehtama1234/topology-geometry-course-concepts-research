Assumption Ledger

This page names the quiet conditions that let a mathematical sentence speak. A theorem, count, model, source claim, or deformation can carry an argument only after its assumptions have been checked in plain language.

How To Use The Ledger

Start with the claim that sounds convincing. Then ask what has to be true for that claim to be allowed. The assumption may be that the same object survived a deformation, that boundary data stayed fixed, that a rule stayed continuous, that all defects were counted, or that the source evidence supports exactly the sentence being written.

The plain check is the important part. It turns an abstract condition into something a reader can inspect: what moved, what stayed fixed, what was counted, what state was modeled, what source was used, and what stronger claim would break the account.

Hidden assumption

Same object through a deformation

Claim: A simplified picture answers the original question only when every simplifying move keeps the same rule-bearing object, so the easier final drawing is still evidence about the starting problem. The plain point is that a conclusion belongs to the original problem only if the condition that defines that problem has not been traded away. A repaired claim names the condition in the sentence itself, so the reader knows what has to be preserved.

Assumption: Every step in the simplification keeps the rule-bearing parts under control: endpoints stay fixed when they define the route problem, gluing rules still identify the same points, obstacles remain forbidden, boundary behavior is preserved, and forbidden crossings do not sneak into the motion. It is the rule that keeps the example honest while the page makes the object easier to see, count, or compare. The condition is not extra polish; it is the part that lets the later reasoning stay attached to the original object.

Why needed: Without this assumption, the proof may solve a cleaner but different problem. The course allows pictures to move only when the question survives the whole motion, not merely when the last picture looks easier to read. The assumption protects the reader from using a good idea in a setting where its main support has disappeared. In plain language, the assumption says which detail is safe to change and which detail is carrying the proof.

Plain check: Can the reader point to the part that moved, name the part that was not allowed to change, and describe one in-between moment to show the forbidden event never happened? A useful check can fail: changing one named condition gives a clear reason the original claim no longer follows. The answer has to name the concrete evidence, not only the course word connected to that evidence.

Breaks if: It breaks if a route slides through an obstacle, a boundary endpoint moves freely, a glued edge is treated as an ordinary edge, or a crossing is erased by letting one curve pass through another. The broken case teaches the limit of the idea better than another correct example would. The repaired explanation then says whether the missing condition can be restored or whether the final claim must become weaker.

Course place: This assumption runs through knot, route, quotient, and surface arguments where a messy drawing is replaced by a cleaner one. It is the check that turns redrawing into reasoning rather than a change of appearance. The linked concepts are deformation, quotient-space, knots-and-links, but the assumption must explain their job before their names are used. This is also why the assumption ledger matters for the whole course: it teaches when a first-principles idea is ready to travel to another problem.

The Assumption Test

Use this page before applying a theorem or trusting a model. If an answer names only the result, ask which assumption gives that result permission to apply. If the assumption cannot be stated without formal language, return to the relevant concept page or theorem contract and rebuild the object from scratch.

A plain explanation does not hide assumptions to feel simple. It states them in everyday language. The failure condition has to be visible: a changed boundary, a discontinuous jump, a missing defect, a bad sign convention, an incomplete state space, or an overstrong source sentence.

Repairing A Missing Assumption

When an answer skips an assumption, add the smallest sentence that makes the permission visible. For a deformation, say what was not allowed to move. For a count, say why the local events are ordinary and why signs share one rule. For a fixed-point claim, say why the rule maps the space back into itself. For a configuration space, say what one point records and what states have been removed.

The repair should not bury the reader in formal prerequisites. It should turn the prerequisite into a checkable sentence. The strongest repair has three parts: the claim being made, the condition that lets the claim speak, and the concrete failure that would make the claim stop applying.

Give no credit for an answer that says only that the theorem assumes the usual conditions. That phrase hides the work. Give credit when the answer names the particular condition in the current problem and shows how the reader could inspect it.

Why This Matters

Assumptions are not bureaucratic details. They are the bridge between a beautiful general statement and the object on the page. The course becomes mathematical when the reader can say why this strip, this route, this field, this surface, this model, or this source sentence is allowed to use the general idea.

This ledger should be used with the change ledger. The change ledger asks what changes and what survives. The assumption ledger asks why that change was legal in the first place. Together they prevent a page from sounding clear while quietly changing the problem.

When reviewing a page, mark the first sentence where a result is used. If the assumption is not visible nearby, the explanation is not ready yet.
