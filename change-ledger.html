Change Ledger

This page makes the central habit of the course explicit: before trusting a picture, count, model, theorem, or source sentence, separate what is allowed to change from what must stay fixed.

How To Read The Ledger

Each row begins with the object, because beginners often start with the visible picture instead of the mathematical thing being studied. The object may be a strip with a gluing rule, a route with obstacles, a cell account of a surface, a map from a space to itself, a vector field, a state space, or a source claim.

The legal-change line states the permission. Some motion, redrawing, refinement, or weakening is allowed, but only inside a contract. The protected-fact line states the evidence that must survive that permission. The why-it-matters line says what mathematical work the surviving evidence can do.

The false move is included because many shallow explanations sound acceptable until the illegal change is named. The reader test is the spoken checkpoint: if the answer cannot say what changes and what stays in everyday language, the formal term has arrived too early.

Change case

Paper strip to global surface fact

Object: The object is the whole strip together with the rule that says which end points are joined. A small rectangle held in the hand is only a local view; it does not by itself tell the reader how the far end is attached.

Legal change: The strip may be bent, carried, squeezed, or inspected in a small window, but the end-joining rule and the route being followed around the strip must stay the same. The strip can look different without becoming a different surface.

Protected fact: The protected fact is what happens to a traveler who goes once around the strip: the traveler either comes back to the same side or to the opposite side. That is a whole-strip fact, not a fact about the neatness of one drawing.

Why it matters: This is the first course warning against trusting a close-up. A local patch can look ordinary while the completed surface has a global rule that controls later cuts, boundary claims, and route claims.

False move: The false move is to flatten the twisted strip into an ordinary band and quietly keep the old conclusion. Flattening is allowed only if it preserves the end relation; otherwise the new picture solves a different problem.

Reader test: Can the reader name the visual changes that are harmless, name the end relation that must be remembered, and predict what a once-around trip reports before and after the supposed simplification?

Change case

Messy route to legal deformation

Object: The object is a route or curve together with the rules attached to it: fixed endpoints, boundary order, missing regions, other curves, or crossing limits. The drawn line alone is not enough information.

Legal change: The route may be nudged into a cleaner position when every moment of the motion respects the same rules. It is not enough for the beginning and ending pictures to look acceptable; the in-between motion must also avoid forbidden regions and forbidden crossings.

Protected fact: The protected fact is the route's relation to the obstacle, boundary order, or other curve that the problem refuses to let it cross. If that relation survives the entire motion, the cleaner picture is allowed to answer the original question.

Why it matters: A deformation proof earns its force by replacing a hard picture with an easier picture while keeping the same question alive. The mathematics is in the rule for the whole motion, not in the visual neatness of the final drawing.

False move: The false move is to clean the drawing by sliding through an obstacle, through another curve, or through a boundary constraint, then announce that the final picture was equivalent all along.

Reader test: Can the reader describe a legal nudge, a forbidden shortcut, the in-between moment where the shortcut breaks the rules, and the exact route relation that the legal nudge preserves?

Change case

Visible crossings to signed evidence

Object: The object is a pair of curves or sheets whose meetings are being counted after the picture has been put into ordinary position. Each meeting must be separate enough that the reader can inspect it by itself.

Legal change: The curves may move through legal deformations that create or remove simple meeting pairs, but the boundary conditions and direction rules must stay fixed. The motion is allowed to change the visible list of crossings without changing the accounting rule.

Protected fact: The protected fact is the signed total. A small legal motion can make two new meetings appear, but if one contributes plus and the other contributes minus, the useful total has not changed.

Why it matters: Raw crossing counts are too sensitive to small picture changes. Signed evidence records the part of the meeting pattern that survives ordinary local accidents: when a legal motion creates an opposite pair, the visible list changes while the net account stays fixed.

False move: The false move is to count every visible crossing as the same kind of evidence and forget that the course count depends on signs, boundary rules, and the possibility of cancelling birth-death pairs.

Reader test: Can the reader isolate one local event, explain where the two crossings come from, assign the two signs using the shared rule, and say why the signed total stayed fixed?

Change case

Cell drawing to Euler characteristic

Object: The object is a surface described by vertices, edges, and faces so the whole surface can be counted. The cells are a bookkeeping plan for the surface; they are not the surface itself.

Legal change: The cutting pattern may be refined, redrawn, or simplified when the new cells still cover the same surface, use the same boundary behavior, and respect the same gluing rules. Adding a diagonal or merging two faces is harmless only under that same-surface check.

Protected fact: The protected fact is the alternating count of vertices, edges, and faces after the bookkeeping changes cancel correctly. Individual numbers can change, but the whole alternating account is designed to survive.

Why it matters: Euler characteristic turns a surface into a stable whole-surface number. It lets the course compare surfaces without being fooled by a busier or cleaner cell drawing that changed only the bookkeeping.

False move: The false move is to trust a convenient cell picture before checking whether it still describes the same surface. A lost glued edge, doubled boundary, or changed surface can make the arithmetic look precise while answering the wrong question.

Reader test: Can the reader name the surface, explain which cell numbers changed under the redraw, and show why the alternating count is still about that same surface rather than a new one?

Change case

Square diagram to quotient surface

Object: The object is the finished surface defined by edge identifications, not the square sheet before the instructions are used. The square is a code for a space; the arrows say which drawn points become one point.

Legal change: The diagram may be redrawn, relabeled, or cut into another convenient code only when the same points are still being identified in the finished space. A new drawing is legal if it preserves the sameness instructions.

Protected fact: The protected fact is the identification rule: which edges match, which directions match, and which apparent boundary points become one point of the completed surface after the gluing instruction is used.

Why it matters: Quotient thinking lets the course build surfaces whose whole behavior is not visible from the flat square alone. A border in the code may stop being a border in the finished surface.

False move: The false move is to reason about distances, sides, or boundaries in the square as if no points had been glued together, or to change arrow directions while pretending the same surface is still present.

Reader test: Can the reader pick two drawn points, decide whether the rule makes them the same point, and say which flat-picture feature no longer deserves to be trusted literally afterward?

Change case

Map rule to fixed-point evidence

Object: The object is a rule that sends each point of a chosen space to an output point, together with the comparison between that rule and the same point staying put. The story is not about motion in general; it is about one rule on one space.

Legal change: The rule may be studied through a graph-and-diagonal comparison when continuity is preserved and every output still lands inside the same target space. The domain, target, and no-jump condition are part of the evidence.

Protected fact: The protected fact is forced self-agreement: a meeting between the graph and the diagonal means at least one point is sent to itself. The claim is existence, not a formula for where the point is.

Why it matters: Shape can force an agreement even when calculation does not find it. After the rule's contract is checked, the course can prove that some self-match must occur without locating it.

False move: The false move is to announce a fixed point before checking that the rule is continuous and maps the space back into itself, or to claim the theorem locates the point when it only forces existence.

Reader test: Can the reader identify the space, the rule, the diagonal of self-agreement, the same-space condition, and the exact conclusion that is earned without claiming location, uniqueness, or a procedure?

Change case

Arrow field to local index

Object: The object is a vector field on a surface, with special attention to one isolated place where the arrow field fails. The small loop around that place is part of the evidence because it lets the reader inspect the surrounding arrows.

Legal change: The arrows may be deformed away from the defect as long as the loop still surrounds the same isolated failure and no extra failure slips through the loop. The comparison to direction must remain readable during the change.

Protected fact: The protected fact is the signed turning number of the arrows around the defect. Individual arrows can be redrawn, but the total turn around the loop survives the permitted cleanup.

Why it matters: Index turns a local failure of arrows into countable evidence. Once each isolated defect has such a count, the course can add local facts instead of relying on a vague story about how the arrows seem to move.

False move: The false move is to count visible equilibria as if they all contribute the same thing, or to ignore whether the calm-looking point is isolated with a surrounding arrow pattern that can be read.

Reader test: Can the reader walk around one defect, say what the arrows do during the walk, explain why the defect stays isolated, and only then decide what number is being added?

Change case

Local defects to Poincare-Hopf

Object: The object is one vector field on one surface, with every isolated defect on that surface included in the same account. The theorem is not about one favorite defect by itself.

Legal change: Defects may move, split, or merge under permitted changes to the field, but the field must remain on the same surface and satisfy the conditions under which the comparison is being made.

Protected fact: The protected fact is the total signed index of all defects. Under the theorem's assumptions, that total must match the Euler characteristic of the same surface carrying the field being studied.

Why it matters: Poincare-Hopf ties local failures to a whole-surface number. It says the defects cannot be discussed as isolated anecdotes once they all live on the same surface and share one account.

False move: The false move is to use the theorem as if it describes every motion path, or to leave out a defect and still compare the partial sum with the whole surface's Euler characteristic.

Reader test: Can the reader name the surface, list the defects being counted, state both sides of the equality, and explain why one missing defect breaks the account for the whole theorem?

Change case

Machine motion to configuration space

Object: The object is the space of allowed states, where one point records one complete position or choice of the system. For a machine, that may include angles, locations, order of parts, and obstacle information.

Legal change: A physical motion becomes a path only if every intermediate state is allowed. Collisions, blocked positions, and impossible choices must be removed before the route question is asked in the model.

Protected fact: The protected fact is the shape of the allowed state space: walls, holes, separated regions, narrow passages, and possible paths between start and target states after impossible states are removed.

Why it matters: Configuration space changes a moving physical story into a shape of possibilities. That static shape can reveal why a motion exists, why it is blocked, or why a drawing of the machine alone has hidden information.

False move: The false move is to draw only the machine in physical space and assume the drawing records every freedom, obstacle, and continuous choice needed for the route problem being modeled.

Reader test: Can the reader say what one state-space point records, which physical situations were removed, and how a path in the space becomes a real gradual motion of the system?

Change case

Source sentence to supported claim

Object: The object is a claim in the companion together with the support available for that exact claim: caption text, lecture context, a visible demonstration, a paper, or a standard textbook source.

Legal change: The sentence may be strengthened, weakened, or moved only according to the evidence actually available and the caveats around that evidence. A caption-backed claim, a course-arc inference, and a background-source claim are different kinds of support.

Protected fact: The protected fact is the boundary between what the lecture record supports, what the companion infers from the course arc, what background sources support, and what still needs human checking.

Why it matters: Source discipline keeps plain explanation honest. The wording helps a reader understand the mathematics without becoming louder than the evidence, especially when captions are auto-generated, partial, missing, or indirect.

False move: The false move is to treat a reference link as proof that every nearby explanatory sentence was stated in the lecture, or to let a strong mathematical background source erase a weaker lecture-specific evidence trail.

Reader test: Can the reader quote the exact claim, name the evidence type behind it, state the strongest version the evidence supports, and rewrite the sentence when the evidence is weaker than the prose?

The Ledger Test

Use this page when an explanation feels fluent but unearned. Ask four questions in order. What is the object? What is allowed to change? What fact is protected while that change happens? What conclusion becomes possible because the fact was protected?

If the answer skips the second question, it may be using an illegal simplification. If it skips the third, it may be giving a visual story without evidence. If it skips the fourth, it may name a correct invariant or theorem without explaining what the idea lets the page prove.

The same test applies to sources. A sentence can change when new evidence is found, but the boundary between caption support, course inference, and background source support must remain visible. That boundary is part of the mathematics because it keeps the explanation accountable.

Repairing A Weak Answer

When an answer fails the ledger test, repair only the missing part. When the object is unclear, add one sentence that says what one point, route, field, cell, or glued edge represents. When the legal change is unclear, add one sentence naming the allowed motion and the forbidden shortcut. When the protected fact is unclear, add one sentence naming the count, side information, equality, path question, or source boundary that survives.

A repair should not become more formal just to sound safer. It should become more checkable. It has to point to the object, act out the allowed change, say what would break the argument, and name the conclusion that the protected fact can support. That is the course habit in its plainest form.

Give no credit for a sentence that only says two pictures are the same, a count is invariant, a theorem applies, or a source supports the claim. Give credit when the sentence explains the permission behind that statement. The permission is the testable part.
