Equilibrium

Equilibrium exists because dynamics often asks where motion must stop or fail. A vector field may be too hard to solve, but the places where its arrows vanish can still be forced, counted, and compared.

Concept Essay

Imagine water flowing across a tabletop. At most points, the water slides in one direction. But at certain spots—near a drain, on top of a hill, or along a ridge—the water does not flow. Those are equilibria: points where the flow field has zero velocity. In a simpler picture, imagine arrows drawn on a piece of paper, each one pointing the direction a particle would move from that spot. An equilibrium is a point with no arrow at all, a blank hole in the field. It is a stop sign in motion.

The real question is not just where the holes are, but what the arrows do nearby. Trace a small circle around the equilibrium and watch the arrows turn as you move. At a drain, they all point inward; at a hill, all point outward; at a saddle, some point in and some point out, like a whirlpool twisted sideways. Each pattern has a signature called the index. The index is a signed count: spiral clockwise as you walk around, and it is +1; spiral counterclockwise, and it is −1; if half the arrows pull left and half pull right, the count twists and becomes a mix. This pattern is the fingerprint of the equilibrium, and it never changes if you nudge the arrows slightly. The dot is silent, but the swirl around it tells the whole story.

Why does the course care? Because the index carries a global constraint. Suppose you draw arrows covering a coffee cup—every point except one gets an arrow pointing somewhere. That equilibrium must exist because the cup is shaped like it is. If you redraw the arrows to move the equilibrium to a different spot, its index stays the same. But here is the limit: if you want to add a second equilibrium, the total count of all indices must match a number locked into the cup's shape, called the Euler characteristic. For a cup (which is topologically a sphere), that number is 2. You can split one saddle into two sources, rearrange them, hide them in the handle—but the total index must stay 2. The shape forces this arithmetic. No arrow field can escape it.

This pays off when you have a motion law too complicated to solve. You do not know the full flow of water across a landscape, but you know the landscape's shape. You can ask: how many equilibria must exist? Where could they be? Could they collide and cancel? Topology answers these without solving the differential equation. A sphere must have at least one equilibrium—this is the hairy ball theorem in disguise. A torus (a doughnut) can have zero—arrows can circle around the hole forever with no still point. A surface with two holes must have equilibria somewhere, and their indices must sum to a specific number. The shape decides what motion is forced to happen. You do not need calculus; you need only geometry.

Here is where intuition often breaks. A beginner thinks an equilibrium is a still point, period. But topology says: only the surrounding arrow pattern decides what it means. A particle starting exactly at an equilibrium stays still forever. A particle starting near a drain-type equilibrium spirals inward; near a hill-type, it spirals outward; near a saddle, it might slide away along one ridge and toward another. The same point—zero velocity—can have completely different meanings depending on whether the nearby arrows attract or repel. This distinction matters because it tells you the equilibrium's role in the whole system. A still point hiding inside a whirlpool is nothing like a still point on top of a hill.

One more surprise: some surfaces demand equilibria to exist, and some do not. On a sphere, every smooth arrow field must have at least one equilibrium. On a torus, you can make arrows flow around the hole forever with no equilibrium at all—the field locks to the doughnut's shape. On a pretzel with two holes, you cannot avoid equilibria; the shape forbids it. A person trying to design a wind pattern on a globe cannot eliminate all calm zones; the globe's shape forces at least one. A person designing wind on a torus can circle the hole forever. This is one of topology's most concrete predictions: the shape of the space decides what motion that space can or cannot hold. It is a constraint written into geometry itself.

The power of equilibrium analysis lies in asking not what happens along each path, but what the shape permits. A landscape is too complicated to trace every raindrop. But the number and type of still points that must exist are written into the terrain itself. An equilibrium is both a local phenomenon—the arrow pattern around it—and a global constraint—its index contributes to a surface-wide total that cannot change. By learning to read the local signatures and count them globally, you learn to predict motion without solving it. That is the core move: from watching every drop to reading the surface's shape. Once you see this, you realize that topology is not abstract—it is the deepest fact about what a shape can do.

Ordinary problem: Imagine you are designing a way to move air around a sphere—perhaps for a model planet or a weather system. You start drawing arrows everywhere, showing which way the air wants to flow. Most of the sphere fills with arrows pointing in a sensible direction. But at some point, you get stuck. You want to continue covering the sphere, but you cannot draw an arrow at a certain spot without creating a knot or a contradiction. Your pencil has nowhere good to go. That spot is an equilibrium: the air does not move there. Now here is the real problem: you did not choose to create that still point. The shape of the sphere forced it into existence. No matter what arrows you draw—no matter how ingenious you are—you cannot cover a sphere with smooth, consistent arrows everywhere. At least one spot must have zero motion. That is not a failure of your design; it is a fact written into the sphere itself. The course shows why this must be true and how to predict it before you even try drawing the arrows. Once you understand equilibria, you can answer questions like: Does a sphere need more still points than a doughnut? Can a person eliminate all calm zones on a planet? The answers come from reading the shape, not from solving the equations.

First Principles

An equilibrium is a point where all motion stops. Imagine a landscape drawn with arrows pointing in the direction a marble would roll from each spot. At an equilibrium, the arrow vanishes—no direction to move. The course cares about equilibria not because they are special solutions, but because the pattern of arrows swirling around them carries hidden information about the whole surface, and that information remains readable without solving the full motion.

Important Detail

The equilibrium itself is just one dot. What matters is the swirl of arrows around it. A source spirals outward, a sink spirals inward, a saddle twists like a whirlpool meeting a current backward. That surrounding pattern—the local index—decides whether the equilibrium is stable, unstable, or mixed, and whether the surface's topology forces it to exist.

Principle Behind It

The index of an equilibrium counts how many times the surrounding arrows rotate around the point as you walk in a circle. A source has index +1, a sink has index +1, a saddle has index −1. The total index across all equilibria on a closed surface equals the surface's Euler characteristic, a number that does not change no matter how you deform the surface smoothly.
