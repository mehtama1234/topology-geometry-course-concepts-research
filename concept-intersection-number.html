Intersection number

Intersection number exists because visible crossings alone are unstable. During a legal motion, crossings can be born or die in pairs. The signed count remembers whether the meetings carry a net obstruction after those pair changes cancel.

Concept Essay

Take two pieces of string and lay them on a table in a tangle where they cross each other several times. Count the crossings with your finger: one, two, three, five. That is a raw count. Now gently slide one string to the side, keeping it flat on the table and never lifting it. Watch what happens at the points where the strings meet. Some crossings may vanish, fused into each other or dissolved by the motion. Some new crossings may spring into existence where the strings no longer avoid each other. Your count jumps: suddenly there are three crossings, or seven, or a different number altogether. Did you find new truth, or did you just redraw the picture? The raw count tells you nothing stable.

This unstable counting is the first clue that something deeper is needed. In science and mathematics, we want numbers that stick around—numbers that do not flip because you redrew your diagram. When you see different counts for what feels like the same object (two tangled strings), it means you are measuring something that depends on how you drew the picture, not something about the strings themselves. The real question is: can we count only the crossings that matter, the ones that cannot be erased by motion, and ignore the rest?

The solution comes from a basic idea: give each string a direction. Draw a small arrow on each string pointing along it. Now at every crossing point, look at the two arrows and ask: in what direction do they rotate around the crossing? Look down at the crossing from above the table. Trace how the first arrow would have to turn to line up with the second arrow. Does it need to rotate clockwise, or counterclockwise? Then do the same for the second arrow. If both arrows rotate the same way to align (both clockwise or both counterclockwise), the crossing is one type. If they rotate opposite ways, the crossing is the other type. Mark the first type with a plus sign and the second type with a minus sign. Do this for all crossings.

Here is where the payoff appears: when you gently slide one string and create two new crossings, those two new crossings always have opposite signs—one plus, one minus. They cancel each other, adding zero to your total. When a motion erases two opposite crossings, they disappear together as a pair, again adding zero to the total by erasure. The sum of all the plus and minus signs across all crossings stays constant. Redraw the tangles however you want—straighten them, coil them, twist them—and if you keep the strings from tearing or gluing, the sum of signed crossings never changes. That sum is the intersection number.

Why does this work so reliably? Because orientation is locked in by geometry, not by accident. Once you decide which way the arrow points along a string, that direction does not change just because you moved the string in space. The rule for assigning signs (clockwise same = plus, clockwise opposite = minus) is built into how the curves are positioned and oriented to each other. When a deformation brings new crossings into being, their signs are already determined by the orientation you set up at the start. They cannot choose to both be plus or both be minus—the geometry forces them to appear as a pair with opposite signs. This is not a sleight-of-hand or a gimmick; it follows from how rotation and orientation work together in space.

Tokieda demonstrates this repeatedly on paper, using two strings with arrows drawn on them, crossing at multiple points. Erase the drawing and redraw the strings using only legal motions—no tearing, no gluing. Count the signed crossings in the new drawing. The total stays the same every time. A small jiggle brings two new crossings (one plus, one minus); their sum adds zero. Another motion erases two opposite crossings, and again the total is unchanged. Watch this happen over and over, and you see that the signed count is not fragile like the raw count—it is anchored to the geometry itself.

This concept opens a door to a technique used throughout topology and geometry. In later chapters, you can count fixed points by treating them as intersections of a graph with a diagonal line. You can count the winding of a vector field by looking at its index around a singularity. In each case, orientation and clean position create a signed count, and that count becomes invariant—a number you can trust because geometry protects it. The value comes not from forgetting the picture, but from reading the geometry inside the picture: the orientation, the position, the way the curves meet—all of this is baked into the signs.

The limits of the concept also teach discipline. Intersection number only works if two conditions are met. First, every crossing must be transverse—the curves must cross at an angle, not touch tangentially. A tangent touch is blurry; the orientation rule does not apply cleanly because the curves are moving in nearly the same direction at that point. Second, both curves must have well-defined orientation everywhere. If you try to put an arrow around an object and find that you cannot do it consistently (the arrow flips or contradicts itself somewhere), then that object has no orientation, and intersection number may not exist or may not survive deformation. These are not exceptions or bugs; they are the honest limits of the method. They tell you: count only what geometry protects.

In the end, intersection number answers a precise and helps question: if two objects move around within the allowed rules, what about their meetings will remain true across all possible motions? The answer is the signed sum—not every crossing visible in one drawing, but every crossing weighted by orientation, where orientation is consistent everywhere and the meeting is clean. That signed total is the intersection number. It carries meaning from one drawing to the next, surviving motion and deformation as a genuine invariant of the geometry. It is the bridge between a visual picture and a reliable number, and the bridge is built from orientation, which lives in the geometry itself, not in arbitrary choice. Understanding why signs must appear in opposite pairs during deformation is the moment when you see that intersection number is worth trusting.

First Principles

When two curves cross on a surface, you could count every crossing point you see. But that count is fragile—wiggle one curve slightly and the number of visible crossings changes. The real answer is to give each curve a direction (like an arrow), then at each crossing point, ask whether the arrows pass each other in the same rotational sense or opposite senses. Assign plus to one kind, minus to the other. The total of all these signed crossings survives any legal wiggle or deformation. That total is the intersection number—a count that captures something permanent about how the curves are bound to meet.

Important Detail

Every crossing point must be transverse, meaning the curves cross sharply at an angle, not tangent to each other. Tangent meetings are blurry and do not have a clear sign under the orientation rule. Also, each curve must have orientation—a direction—chosen consistently all the way along. Without consistent orientation, you have no rule for assigning plus or minus at each crossing, and the count falls apart. The discipline of requiring clear position and consistent direction is what lets the count survive any deformation.

Principle Behind It

At each transverse crossing of two oriented curves, compare the tangent direction vectors at that point. If rotating the first vector to the second vector goes the same rotational way around the crossing (both clockwise or both counterclockwise), assign plus. If the rotations go opposite ways, assign minus. Sum all the signs from all crossings. This sum is invariant: any continuous deformation that keeps crossings transverse preserves the total.

Failure Mode

Counting crossings before explaining signs loses the evidence. The sign records how oriented pieces meet inside the surrounding space. Without that direction information, cancellation may be wishful arithmetic rather than a mathematical reason. The count must say which meetings are real evidence and which appear only as canceling pairs.
