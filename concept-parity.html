Parity

Parity exists because sometimes the exact number is too sensitive, but evenness or oddness is stable. If legal changes create or remove events two at a time, the parity cannot change, even while the visible count changes.

Concept Essay

Imagine two ropes tangled in knots, and you want to separate them by sliding the knots around without cutting. As you work, you count the number of times the ropes cross each other. Each time you slide a knot past a crossing, the crossing count changes—but it never changes by one. It always changes by two, or by zero. If you start with three crossings (odd), you can reach one crossing or five crossings, but you will never reach two, four, or zero. The oddness is trapped. This is parity: a count that refuses to flip between odd and even.

Take a sheet of paper and fold it. Count the layers as you go. At the start you have one layer (odd). Fold once in half: now you have two layers where you fold. Keep folding, each time doubling the layers in the folded region. When you unfold the paper completely and count all the crease segments you created, that count depends on how many folds you made. The number of creases has a locked relationship to the number of folds. It will never jump from odd to even, because each fold creates creases in a pattern that always changes the count by an even number. The parity is baked in.

The wrong intuition is that if a count can change, it can change to anything. Watch someone unfamiliar with parity try to count the sides of a tangle: they count ten, then twelve, then fifteen, and they think any number is possible. But parity stops them cold. If the count is always even after each allowed move, then ten and fifteen are both impossible—only even numbers can appear. That restriction comes from a single rule: every legal rearrangement affects the count by two. Not all situations obey this rule. Some allow moves that change a count by one, and then parity means nothing. The strength comes from proving that the moves you face always happen in pairs, never one at a time.

Here is how parity works in topology. Imagine a curve drawn on a coffee cup, and you want to know whether the curve can be shrunk to nothing without tearing the cup or the curve. As you try to shrink the curve, you move it around and smooth out bends. Suppose the curve crosses itself at certain points, and you count the crossings. Each legal simplification—pulling the curve taut along the surface—changes the crossing count by two, or leaves it alone. This happens because crossings come in matched pairs when you perform a local move: either you untangle two strands and eliminate two crossings together, or you keep the crossings locked. You never eliminate exactly one crossing. So if your curve starts with an odd crossing count, it will always have an odd crossing count, no matter how much you smooth and rearrange. This means if the smooth version of the curve has zero crossings (even), then the original tangle had to be already untangled or it belonged to a different class. Parity splits curves into two tribes that cannot pass between each other.

The arithmetic behind parity is direct: if a count starts at five (odd) and you add two, you get seven (still odd). Add two again, you get nine. Subtract two, you get seven. No matter how many pairs you add or remove, an odd count stays odd. This is not mystical—it is basic arithmetic. Parity is the shadow of this rule, cast onto the world of shapes and tangles. A quantity is invariant under allowed operations if and only if those operations always change it by the same multiple. When every operation changes a count by two or zero, the parity is locked. This locking is absolute: no sequence of operations can flip an odd number to an even number, or vice versa, because you are only adding multiples of two.

Parity plays two roles in topology. First, it is a checkpoint: when you want to prove two configurations cannot be the same, you count something and show that one configuration has an even count while the other is odd. Since parity cannot change, they are locked into different classes forever. Second, parity can signal that something must exist. If you start with an odd count and the only way to eliminate the count entirely is to reach zero (even), then parity blocks that path. An obstruction by parity is weak—it only rules out one value—but in the right context, that one blocked state is enough to prove a fact about the shape.

The limit of parity is sharp: it only works when moves happen in pairs. The moment one legal operation can change a count by one, the even-or-odd fortress crumbles. A chess position evolves by single moves, one piece at a time, so parity of the piece count is not protected (you can capture one piece and change the count by one). A knot's crossing count changes one-by-one in some rearrangements, so raw crossing count is not invariant. This is why parity is often a first step in topology: it holds its ground within its boundary but fails beyond it. As the course moves forward, signed counts and oriented crossings replace bare parity, tracking not just odd or even but the direction and cancellation of each event. Parity is the foundation on which these finer instruments rest.

In the everyday world beyond topology, parity appears wherever events come in pairs. A choreography that flips left-and-right symmetry in each move will oscillate: odd flips after move one, even after move two, odd after move three. A circuit with toggle switches can flip in and out of on or off based on whether the total number of switch flips is odd or even. A system of weights and balances where every transaction involves moving two weights will preserve the oddness of the remaining weight on one side. Parity is not a topology gimmick—it is a fact about the arithmetic of doubling. Topology latches onto it because tangles, crossings, and holes have obvious pairings built in.

First Principles

Parity asks whether a count is even or odd. Many physical actions in tangles, folds, and tangles occur in matched pairs. When exact numbers change with each motion but the odd-or-even status stays locked, parity becomes the invariant—the property that refuses to flip no matter what rearrangements you perform.

Important Detail

Parity protects a count only when every allowed move changes it by an even number: zero, two, four, six, or any multiple of two. If even one legal operation can change the count by one, the protection fails and any parity is possible. The shield only works when all moves come in pairs.

Principle Behind It

If every permitted operation changes a quantity by a multiple of two, that quantity's parity is invariant under all sequences of operations. Starting parity determines an unchangeable class: odd numbers stay odd, even numbers stay even, because adding or subtracting multiples of two preserves parity.
