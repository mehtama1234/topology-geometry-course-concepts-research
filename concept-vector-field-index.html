Vector field index

Vector-field index exists because solving a differential equation is often too much to ask. The index extracts a small but durable fact from the arrow pattern near an equilibrium: how the arrows turn around the failure point.

Concept Essay

Vector-field index is the dynamics version of signed counting. A vector field assigns an arrow to each point. Where the arrow vanishes, there is an equilibrium or defect. The index records how the nearby arrows turn around that defect. It is local evidence, but it is designed so it survives appropriate changes in the field.

The first-principles picture is to walk once around a small loop surrounding the defect and watch the arrows turn. If the arrows make a full turn, or reverse their turning in a saddle-like way, that local behavior receives a signed count. The center point may have no arrow, but the surrounding arrows reveal how the field fails there. The index is the record of that failure, not a label attached to the dot itself.

The concept matters because it lets topology speak about differential equations without solving them. Sources, sinks, saddles, and other local patterns may move or change under deformation, but their signed total can be constrained by the surface. Poincare-Hopf is the payoff: the sum of local indices equals Euler characteristic. A fact about arrows becomes a fact about the shape carrying them.

This turns a hard analytic question into a topological one: not where every trajectory goes, but what failures the whole surface requires. A differential equation may be impossible to solve explicitly, but its arrow field may still be forced to have defects. The course uses index to make that weaker-looking conclusion precise.

The important caution is that the index belongs to isolated defects with a readable surrounding arrow pattern. If a whole curve of arrows vanishes, or if the local picture is not cleaned up, the count may not yet be available. Generic position and deformation prepare the field so the local evidence can be counted and then added across the surface. A beginner should therefore inspect the small loop, not only the named equilibrium: what do the nearby arrows do during one trip around the defect?

First Principles

Put a little arrow at each point of a surface. Where the arrow vanishes or the arrow pattern cannot be read as a normal direction, isolate that place and walk around a small loop enclosing it. The index records the signed turning of nearby arrows during that walk. It is not a count of how many defects are visible; it is a local account of how one defect behaves.

Important Detail

The defect must be isolated and the loop must contain only that defect. If two defects sit inside the same loop, or if the field fails along a whole curve, the single local index has not been separated cleanly enough.

Principle Behind It

A local failure of a field becomes usable evidence only after its surrounding arrow behavior is turned into a signed count that can be added with other local counts.

Beginner Trap

The trap is to classify equilibria by appearance alone. The index is a signed turning count, and different-looking local pictures may have the same index while similar-looking pictures may behave differently under deformation.

Course Role

Index is the dynamics version of signed intersection. It lets Poincare-Hopf connect local equilibria to Euler characteristic. The concept proves that a local arrow defect can carry exactly the kind of signed information the earlier intersection theory taught us to trust.

Operation: Walk around each defect on a small loop and count how the nearby arrows turn. The operation reads the failure from its surroundings instead of only naming the zero.

Protected fact: The protected fact is the signed local turning count, which can move with the defect and enter a global sum. The count records how motion fails locally.

Breaks if: It breaks if the defect is not isolated, or if the surrounding arrow direction is not read consistently around the loop. Then the local count cannot be trusted.
