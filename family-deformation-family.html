Deformation arguments

The human problem is that the original picture is often too tangled to reason about directly, but an uncontrolled simplification can answer the wrong question. The family asks how to change the picture while keeping the exact data the problem cares about.

Method Essay

Deformation arguments are the course's way of replacing a hard object by an easier one without losing the question. The first step is not to move anything. The first step is to state the contract: what may bend, what must stay attached, which crossings are forbidden, which endpoints stay fixed, and which boundaries still count as boundaries. Only after that contract is clear does motion become a proof method.

The family matters because many topological questions are impossible to settle from the first drawing. A tangled drawing may hide a simple answer; a simple-looking drawing may hide an obstruction. Deformation gives the reader permission to simplify, but only under rules that preserve the feature being tested. This is why the endpoint of a deformation proof is not evidence by itself. The evidence is the whole legal journey from the original picture to the easier one.

From first principles, deformation separates appearance from obligation. Appearance is the visible wiggle, bend, angle, or placement. Obligation is the part the problem refuses to let go: boundary order, no crossing, one-sidedness, a signed total, or the meaning of a map. A deformation proof moves the appearance while guarding the obligation. If the obligation survives, the easier picture can answer the harder one.

This family connects the early disk-path puzzle to later vector-field cleanup. In the disk puzzle, the protected data include endpoint order and the no-crossing rule. In surface surgery, the protected data include the surface being studied and the effect of each cut or gluing step. In vector-field cleanup, the protected data include the total index unless a controlled event explains how local defects change. The proof is not saying that all drawings are equally good. It is saying that the allowed drawings form a connected range of possibilities, and the protected fact stays the same throughout that range. The reader should therefore ask for the route, not only the before-and-after pictures.

The mathematical principle is plain: if every allowed step preserves the relevant fact, then the final easy picture can speak for the original hard picture. The danger is equally plain: one illegal step can make the final picture irrelevant. Deformation works because its freedom is disciplined.

Purpose

Replace a difficult object by an easier one while preserving the answer.

First Principles

Start by naming the legal moves and the fixed data. Then move the object through those moves until it is easier to inspect. If endpoints, boundaries, gluing rules, forbidden crossings, side behavior, or a protected count survive every step, the simplified picture speaks for the original picture and not merely for itself.

How It Works

A deformation proof has four parts: the starting object, the contract of allowed motion, the protected feature, and the simpler endpoint. The proof fails if any part is left unnamed or unchecked. The endpoint is convincing only because the route to it was legal and carried the protected feature all the way.

Course Examples

The disk-connection puzzle, surface classification by surgery, intersection invariance, and the treatment of vector-field defects all use this family. In each case, motion is not a side effect; it is the proof method.

Failure Mode

The common failure is to simplify by a move that the problem does not permit. Cutting a loop, sliding an endpoint, passing through an obstacle, or changing a boundary condition may make the picture easier while solving the wrong problem.

Method Playbook

Setup: Start with the original object, not the cleaned-up target picture. Mark the fixed data before any simplification: endpoints, boundary pieces, obstacles, gluing rules, side choices, forbidden crossings, and the question being protected. If the fixed data are unclear, the motion has no proof value.

Move: Move the object continuously through only the allowed changes. Check the in-between states, not only the start and finish. After each kind of move, say why the protected fact has not changed and why the move did not cross a forbidden event.

Payoff: The final simpler picture answers the original problem because the legal route to it carried the same question all the way across. The simplified picture is evidence only through that route; without the route, it is just another drawing.

Failure: The method fails when the easy picture is reached by crossing through a forbidden object, moving fixed data, skipping intermediate states, or silently changing what sameness means. Then the final picture may be readable but it answers a different question.

Reader test: Can the reader describe the full legal route, name one tempting illegal shortcut, and state what fact was preserved along the route at every step?

Method Contract

Input: A hard picture whose exact drawing is less important than the rule it carries: a route, boundary order, side behavior, gluing instruction, forbidden crossing, or count. The input must name the thing being moved and the data that are not allowed to move. A path with fixed endpoints, for example, is not the same input as a loose curve.

Action: State the legal moves before moving anything. Then move the object through only those moves, checking the in-between states as well as the final picture. Each move has to preserve the named question: the proof may bend, slide, or redraw the picture, but it may not win by cutting, crossing forbidden matter, or changing the setup.

Protected evidence: The protected evidence is the fact that survives the whole motion: same endpoints, same boundary data, same route relation, same gluing, same side behavior, or same invariant. The evidence is not the cleaned-up endpoint. The evidence is the survival of the named fact during the entire legal journey.

Output: The output is a simpler picture that can answer the original question because the legal path to it preserved that question. The result is the original problem seen in a readable form, with a record of why the simplification did not rewrite the problem.

Failure test: Reject the argument if it cuts, glues, passes through forbidden matter, moves fixed data, skips an in-between state, or changes the meaning of sameness. One illegal step disconnects the final picture from the starting problem, even if the final picture looks convincing.

Concepts in this family

Concept

Generic position

Generic position exists because special coincidences are too brittle to reason from. If three things meet at exactly one point, or a curve just kisses another curve, a tiny nudge can change the picture. The course wants arguments that survive tiny nudges, so it first moves the picture away from accidental perfection.

Open
Concept

Deformation

Deformation exists because exact drawings often hide the answer. If stretching a curve does not change the question, then a complicated curve can be replaced by a simpler one. The purpose is not to make a cleaner-looking picture; it is to preserve the right fact while throwing away distracting measurement.

Open
Concept

Invariant

An invariant exists to give memory to a changing picture. When a shape bends or a map moves, most visible details change. The invariant is the part chosen carefully enough that it does not change under the allowed motion.

Open
Concept

Topology and geometry

The distinction exists because not every question needs the same kind of information. Geometry asks how much, how long, how curved, or at what angle. Topology asks how connected, how many holes, which routes are blocked, and what survives bending.

Open
Concept

Surgery

Surgery exists because classification needs controlled operations, not passive looking. By removing a known piece and gluing in another, the course can expose handles, crosscaps, and boundaries and can compare surfaces through repeatable moves that a reader can audit.
