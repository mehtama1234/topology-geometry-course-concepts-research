Drawing and embedding arguments

The human problem is to know whether one object can sit inside another without forbidden collisions. A drawing may suggest an answer, but this family asks for a reason that covers every drawing and every legal attempt. It replaces drawing frustration with an audit of the object, the room, and the forbidden moves.

Method Essay

Drawing and embedding arguments ask whether there is enough room for required connections. A failed drawing is not enough, because a better drawing may exist. The family therefore separates the object that must be placed from the space that must receive it. Which endpoints are fixed? Which crossings are forbidden? Can one strand pass through another? Does the surrounding surface have holes or handles that provide alternate routes?

This is where knots, links, planar graphs, path puzzles, and intersection arguments meet. Each problem asks whether a relation can be removed by legal motion or whether it belongs to the shape of the situation. A crossing on the page may be accidental. A linking relation or forced intersection may survive every attempt to redraw.

From first principles, embedding is a room test. The object has demands: these points must connect, this loop must close, these strands may not pass through each other. The surrounding space has allowances: routes around holes, extra dimensions, boundaries, or a flat page with no overpass. The proof asks whether the allowances can satisfy the demands without violating the rules.

This family also teaches the difference between a drawing and an obstruction. A drawing is one attempt. An obstruction is a reason every legal attempt fails. That reason may be boundary order in a disk, linking in space, a signed intersection count, or a shortage of dimensions. The course keeps returning to this distinction because visual failure alone is too weak.

The method is not testing drawing skill. It is proving that all legal drawings face the same constraint. The surface or surrounding space supplies only certain routes, and sometimes those routes are not enough. When that happens, the failure belongs to the topology of the placement problem.

Purpose

Ask whether required connections, loops, or strands can live in the chosen surrounding space without forbidden crossings, lost over-under data, or illegal pass-through moves.

First Principles

Separate the object's required connections from the room supplied by the surrounding surface or space. If every legal placement would force a forbidden meeting, the obstruction belongs to the topology, not to a bad drawing. The object and the receiving room must both be named before the placement question is meaningful.

How It Works

Embedding arguments track crossings, over-under information, winding, linking, endpoint order, and surface room. They ask whether deformation can remove intersections or whether a count proves that some relation survives every allowed placement in the space under study. The proof must explain why the obstruction defeats all legal placements, not just one sketch.

Course Examples

Path puzzles in a disk, planar graph questions, knots and links, and intersection theory all belong here. The family teaches the reader to distinguish accidental crossings from forced ones by checking what legal motion can remove. Later, the same habit helps separate a visible meeting from a signed intersection that cannot be dismissed.

Failure Mode

The common failure is to confuse a failed attempt with a proof of impossibility. A topological obstruction must show that every legal attempt fails, not only the first drawing tried or the first plausible arrangement. A second failure is forgetting data that the flat drawing hides, such as over-under order or the surface that supplies the routes.

Method Playbook

Setup: Start by separating the required object from the surrounding room: which points connect, which loops link, which crossings are forbidden, and what surface or space carries the placement. The room is part of the problem because a plane, sphere, torus, or three-dimensional space permits different routes.

Move: Try legal placements while tracking route relations, over-under information, crossings, winding, endpoint order, and forbidden passages that cannot be erased by allowed motion. The move tests the placement relation, not drawing skill or the neatness of one sketch.

Payoff: The result is either a legal placement or a proof that every legal placement must encounter the same obstruction. A real obstruction defeats all legal attempts, not only the first drawing that comes to mind, because it is tied to the relation the placement must preserve.

Failure: The method fails when one bad drawing is treated as proof, when spatial information such as over-under order is lost in a flat picture, or when the surrounding surface is quietly changed. The missing data may be the whole point, especially for knots, links, and route problems.

Reader test: Can the reader say why every legal attempt is blocked, rather than only why one attempted drawing failed, name the surrounding room, and identify the placement relation that survives redrawings?

Concepts in this family

Concept

Planar graphs

Planarity exists because connection problems are also space problems. A graph may only say which dots must be joined, but drawing it on a plane adds a constraint: the routes must share a surface without forbidden crossings.

Open
Concept

Knots and links

Knots and links exist in the course because a curve in space can remember how it is embedded. Length, roundness, and exact position can change, yet the loop may still be unable to become a plain circle by legal motion.

Open
Concept

Winding and linking

Winding and linking exist because going around something can be a stable relationship. A loop around a post can stretch and wiggle, but it cannot stop going around the post unless it crosses the post or breaks.

Open
Concept

Dual pictures

Duality exists because the first picture of a problem may hide the useful count. Turning regions into dots or borders into connections can reveal a structure that was present but hard to see in the original drawing.

Open
Concept

Manifold

Manifold exists as the course's word for a space where small neighborhoods behave like ordinary room, even when the total space does not. This lets the lectures put curves, surfaces, maps, and vector fields in settings that are locally manageable but still carry holes, side reversal, boundary behavior, or whole-surface constraints.

Open
Concept

Intersection number

Intersection number exists because visible crossings alone are unstable. During a legal motion, crossings can be born or die in pairs. The signed count remembers whether the meetings carry a net obstruction after those pair changes cancel.
