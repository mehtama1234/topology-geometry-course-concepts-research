Tadashi Tokieda: Topology & Geometry

Build a source-backed companion for Tadashi Tokieda's Topology & Geometry course that treats the course as a way of thinking, not as a list of terms. For every lecture, theme, subtheme, and paper-style family of ideas, explain the point from first principles in plain everyday language. Start with the human problem: what can we know about a shape, motion, or constraint when exact measurement is the wrong tool? Then show the mathematical move: deform the object, keep track of what cannot change, count the right thing, and use that count to force a conclusion. Avoid assuming prior math knowledge. Avoid machine-learning, benchmark, causal-inference, optimization, and systems jargon. Avoid loose filler and stock teaching phrases. The result should make the important mathematical principle feel necessary: what detail matters, why it matters, how it connects to the rest of the course, and what kind of problem it lets a person solve.

Core Course Move

Theme

See by bending without tearing

The course keeps asking what remains true after a shape is stretched, nudged, or redrawn. The everyday question is whether a messy object can be changed without changing the problem. Exact size and angle are not thrown away; they are set aside only when the question is about a route, boundary, gluing rule, or count that survives the allowed motion. Theme role for See by bending without tearing: The Mobius strip makes the need for global thinking visible. The disk-connection puzzle turns deformation into a proof method. Surface classification uses deformation to reduce complicated surfaces to standard parts. Intersection number, fixed points, and vector-field index later depend on the same promise: move the object, but keep the protected answer. The course uses See by bending without tearing to keep the course-wide idea concrete: each later page has to show the carrier, the legal change, the protected fact, and the claim limit.

Count the thing that cannot disappear

Many arguments in the lectures replace a complicated picture with a stubborn count: holes, crossings, turns, regions, signs, or boundary pieces. The plain problem is that the visible picture changes too easily, so the course needs a receipt for what did not change. The count is valuable only after the page explains which moves threaten it and why the chosen count survives those moves. Theme role for Count the thing that cannot disappear: The early paper examples make survival concrete. Euler characteristic turns a cut-up surface into a stable number. Intersection number shows how signs protect a count under deformation. Poincare-Hopf later uses the same idea when local vector-field indices add to a whole-surface number. For Count the thing that cannot disappear, the plain boundary is part of the meaning: the theme can guide attention only as far as the object, allowed move, and surviving evidence remain visible.

Use small facts to force whole-shape facts

A surface may look harmless when seen in tiny neighborhoods, just as one small patch of a strip, field, or path diagram may look ordinary. The real question is whether all those small choices can fit together across the finished object. The surprise is that many local rules can add up to a global demand on the whole shape. Theme role for Use small facts to force whole-shape facts: The theme starts with one-sidedness, grows through orientation and surface classification, and becomes numerical in Euler characteristic and Poincare-Hopf. By the dynamics lectures, local equilibria and their indices are forced to obey a global surface count rather than acting independently. Theme

Understand the ordinary case first

The course often moves away from special coincidences: tangencies, perfect alignments, double accidents, or delicate symmetries. The ordinary case is easier to reason about because accidents have been removed in a legal way. This matters because a proof should not depend on a perfect accident that disappears as soon as the picture is nudged, redrawn, or measured a little differently. Theme role for Understand the ordinary case first: Generic position is present whenever Tokieda makes intersections clean, counts them with signs, or treats equilibria as isolated defects. It is also present in the physical demonstrations: the point is not the exact accidental drawing, but the behavior that survives a small nudge. Read Understand the ordinary case first as a course reading rule, not a heading. It helps the reader see why a drawing, theorem, source, or application is being used at that point.

Make pictures carry reasons

The lectures use drawings heavily, but a drawing has to do proof work. A useful course picture is not decoration or a memory aid by itself. It carries proof only when it shows the object, the allowed moves, the forbidden moves, and the quantity or relation being protected. A careful reader can point to the reason in the picture. Treat shape as a machine

A shape can force motion, block a motion, or make an outcome unavoidable. The plain idea is that possible states form a space, and the shape of that space may leave no legal escape. Topology and geometry reach into mechanisms, knots, surfaces, and physical puzzles because they can describe possible behavior before every detail is solved. Theme role for Treat shape as a machine: This begins with physical strips and path puzzles, passes through fixed points as intersections with the diagonal, and reaches dynamical systems where vector fields and equilibria are constrained by topology. Applications late in the course show the same method outside pure surface examples. First-Principles Concepts

Concept

Generic position

Imagine two pencil lines drawn on a piece of rubber. You can draw them so they cross at a corner where four floor tiles meet. But stretch the rubber a little, and that perfect corner alignment vanishes—the crossing slides into the middle of one tile, and the accident is gone. Generic position means shifting or tweaking your setup just enough to destroy these accidental alignments. Once the accident is gone, intersections happen one at a time, contacts are crisp, and you can count what is actually there without coincidences hiding the real structure.

Open
Concept

Deformation

Deformation is the controlled bending of shapes—paper, curves, maps, surfaces—without cutting or gluing. It strips away exact measurements to find what stays the same when you bend, stretch, or slide an object within the rules you set. This gives you a tool to replace a tangled picture with a cleaner one while keeping the fact you actually care about. The core idea: some features of a shape are fragile and break under any tiny wiggle, while others survive bending, stretching, and sliding no matter how extreme. Learning to tell them apart is the whole game.

Open
Concept

Invariant

An invariant is a chosen fact that survives exactly the changes your problem allows. Start by naming those allowed moves: bending paper, redrawing curves, breaking apart and reconnecting cells, sliding paths, or deforming maps. Then check: does the fact stay the same before and after each move? If it changes, no sequence of legal moves can connect those two situations. That is why the fact matters—it becomes your tool for proving something is impossible. You build an invariant by identifying what remains locked while everything else flows and shifts around it. The invariant does not describe the whole picture; it captures exactly enough to answer whether two states belong to the same family.

Concept

Topology and geometry

Geometry measures what is happening right now at each point: how far, how wide, how sharp the curve, what the slope is. Topology identifies what facts about an object stay true even when you bend, stretch, squish, or redraw it without tearing or pasting. Both are ways of asking about the same object—one captures the local measurements you see this moment, the other captures what cannot be erased by legal deformation.

Concept

Euler characteristic

Every time you redraw a surface by adding lines or changing borders, the vertices, edges, and faces all shift. But if you count them in an alternating way—vertices minus edges plus faces—that sum stays the same no matter how you redraw. This stable number belongs to the surface itself, not to any particular drawing of it. It reveals which surfaces are fundamentally different from each other even if they can be bent and stretched to look alike.

Concept

Triangulation and cell decomposition

When you want to understand a soft, bendable surface, the move is to cut it mentally into pieces you can count. Triangles work because once you name each corner, record which edges link to which corners, and list which triangles sit between them, you have replaced an infinite object with a finite ledger. The claim is not that the surface *is* made of triangles—it is not. The claim is that a named decomposition with a gluing record lets you ask questions and get answers where the infinity would have stopped you cold before.

Concept

Planar graphs

A graph is dots connected by lines. The genuine question is whether you can draw those lines on a flat sheet without any crossing. Here's the trap: a drawing where lines cross might just mean you placed things poorly, not that it cannot be done. Planarity asks which graphs can survive any rearrangement without crossings—it separates what the connection pattern forces from what a flat surface actually permits.

Open
Concept

Knots and links

A knot is a closed loop of rope or cord that cannot become an ordinary circle by bending and sliding alone, without cutting or passing through itself. The deep question is not whether it looks messy on paper, but whether certain loop configurations carry structural memory that survives all legal motion. This turns the problem into a precise rule: what stays locked when everything else is allowed to move? The answer requires you to understand that a knot is not defined by how it appears in a drawing—it is defined by whether there exists any sequence of allowed moves that can untangle it. This distinction between appearance and structure is the whole point.

Open
Concept

Winding and linking

Take a loop of rope and place it around a post, then try to remove it without lifting the loop. You cannot—the loop has trapped a relationship between two paths. Winding counts how many times a closed curve goes around an obstacle, and this count survives reshaping, stretching, or bending. Linking extends this to two loops: if they are tangled, no continuous motion through space can untangle them without one passing through the other. Topology asks whether certain relationships can persist through every allowed deformation, independent of how shapes look.
