Assumption Ledger

This page names the quiet conditions that let a mathematical sentence speak. A theorem, count, model, source claim, or deformation is useful only after its assumptions have been checked in plain language.

How To Use The Ledger

Start with the claim that sounds convincing. Then ask what has to be true for that claim to be allowed. The assumption may be that the same object survived a deformation, that boundary data stayed fixed, that a rule stayed continuous, that all defects were counted, or that the source evidence supports exactly the sentence being written.

The plain check is the important part. It turns an abstract condition into something a reader can inspect: what moved, what stayed fixed, what was counted, what state was modeled, what source was used, and what stronger claim would break the account.

Hidden assumption

Same object through a deformation

Claim: A simplified picture answers the original question because the simplification did not change what the question was asking about.

Assumption: Every step in the simplification keeps the same mathematical object for the purpose of the question: endpoints, gluing rules, obstacles, boundary behavior, and forbidden crossings stay under control.

Why needed: Without this assumption, the proof may solve a cleaner but different problem. The course allows pictures to move only when the protected question survives the motion.

Plain check: Can the reader point to the thing that moved and also name the part of the setup that was not allowed to change?

Breaks if: It breaks if a route slides through an obstacle, a boundary endpoint moves freely, or a glued edge is treated as an ordinary edge.

Course place: This assumption runs through knot, route, quotient, and surface arguments where a messy drawing is replaced by a cleaner one.

Hidden assumption

Boundary data is fixed

Claim: A crossing, route obstruction, or impossibility survives simplification because the boundary information defining the problem stayed fixed.

Assumption: The boundary order, marked points, endpoint positions, or side labels that define the problem remain fixed while the inside of the picture changes.

Why needed: Many topological obstructions live in the boundary data. If the boundary can be rearranged, the obstruction may disappear because the problem itself has been changed.

Plain check: Can the reader say which data live on the edge of the picture and why those data are part of the rulebook?

Breaks if: It breaks if endpoints are swapped, boundary labels are ignored, or a path is allowed to leave the region it was supposed to stay inside.

Course place: This assumption appears when crossings are forced by endpoint order and when boundary orientation gives signs their shared reference.

Hidden assumption

Continuity has not been lost

Claim: A rule, path, deformation, or motion cannot jump past the obstruction because every intermediate state must still be accounted for.

Assumption: The change happens without sudden jumps: nearby inputs remain nearby outputs, and a moving system passes through intermediate states rather than teleporting.

Why needed: Continuity is the reason shape can force behavior. If jumps are allowed, a route can skip a wall, a fixed-point argument can avoid the diagonal, and a deformation can cross a forbidden event.

Plain check: Can the reader describe what the intermediate states are and why the motion cannot skip from before to after in one step?

Breaks if: It breaks if a map tears the space, a path jumps across a missing region, or a model lets the system pass through an impossible state.

Course place: This assumption supports deformation, fixed-point, and configuration-space arguments whenever a path or rule is used as evidence.

Hidden assumption

The counted meetings are ordinary

Claim: A signed count of intersections can be used as evidence because the meetings have been prepared one at a time.

Assumption: The objects meet cleanly rather than in a fragile coincidence such as tangency, overlap, or several events happening at exactly the same place.

Why needed: Signs and cancellations need meetings that can be inspected one at a time. Ordinary position makes the local event simple enough to count and stable enough to survive small legal moves.

Plain check: Can the reader isolate one meeting, assign its sign, and explain what nearby legal motion would do to it?

Breaks if: It breaks if curves lie on top of each other, touch without crossing, or pass through a special accident that has not been separated into ordinary events.

Course place: This assumption sits behind generic position, intersection number, and clean-meeting source families whenever crossings are counted.

Hidden assumption

Signs have a shared direction rule

Claim: Plus and minus contributions can be added and trusted because they use one shared direction rule across the object.

Assumption: The space, boundary, or local setup carries a consistent direction convention so that a plus sign in one place is comparable to a plus sign somewhere else.

Why needed: Signed arithmetic only works when the signs belong to the same rulebook. Without a shared direction rule, the symbols may look precise while adding unrelated local choices.

Plain check: Can the reader say where the direction convention comes from and whether it can be carried across the object being used?

Breaks if: It breaks if orientation is assumed on a surface that does not support it, or if each crossing receives a sign from a private local choice.

Course place: This assumption matters for boundary orientation, intersection number, turning counts, and index comparisons across one shared object.

Hidden assumption

All defects are included

Claim: A total index or Poincare-Hopf comparison constrains the field because all local defects belong to one complete account.

Assumption: Every isolated defect of the vector field on the relevant surface is counted, and the field satisfies the theorem's boundary or surface conditions.

Why needed: A whole-surface theorem compares a complete local account with a whole-surface number. Leaving out one defect makes the local side no longer match the object being studied.

Plain check: Can the reader list the surface, the field, every defect being counted, and the surface number the total is compared with?

Breaks if: It breaks if a defect is hidden outside the drawing, if boundary behavior is ignored, or if the surface used for Euler characteristic is not the surface carrying the field.

Course place: This assumption is central to vector-field index and Poincare-Hopf explanations whenever local defects are added globally.

Vector field indexPoincare-Hopf theoremEuler characteristic

Hidden assumption

One point records one full state

Claim: A configuration-space path represents a real physical motion because each point records one complete allowed state.

Assumption: Each point of the configuration space records all data needed for one complete allowed state, and every forbidden state has been removed.

Why needed: A motion problem becomes topology only when the state space is faithful. Missing freedoms or missing obstacles make paths in the model lie about the physical system.

Plain check: Can the reader say what one point records, what has been removed, and what a continuous path means in the original situation?

Breaks if: It breaks if collisions are left inside the state space, if a necessary angle or position is not recorded, or if two different physical states are accidentally treated as one.

Course place: This assumption supports configuration-space and product-space modeling in the later motion examples.

Hidden assumption

The rule maps the space to itself

Claim: A fixed-point theorem or graph-and-diagonal argument can be used because the rule stays inside one space.

Assumption: The rule takes every allowed input point back into the same space, and the continuity and boundary conditions required by the argument are still true.

Why needed: A fixed point is self-agreement inside one space. If the rule leaves the space or changes the setup, the diagonal comparison no longer asks the right question.

Plain check: Can the reader name the space, name the rule, and explain why every output is still an allowed point of that same space?

Breaks if: It breaks if outputs land outside the region, if the rule jumps, or if the boundary behavior used by the theorem has been changed.

Course place: This assumption supports fixed-point pages, Brouwer contracts, and graph-and-diagonal explanations where self-agreement is forced.

Fixed pointsBrouwer fixed-point theoremConfiguration space

Hidden assumption

A cell count describes the same surface

Claim: Euler characteristic survives changing the cell decomposition because the new cell account still describes the same surface.

Assumption: The vertices, edges, and faces form a valid account of the same surface before and after the cutting or refinement is changed.

Why needed: Euler characteristic is a whole-surface ledger, not a reward for one drawing. The count survives because artificial internal bookkeeping changes cancel while the surface remains the same.

Plain check: Can the reader say what surface is being counted and why the new cells still describe that surface?

Breaks if: It breaks if a boundary is lost, a glued edge is counted twice incorrectly, or the drawing is changed into a different surface while keeping the old label.

Course place: This assumption supports triangulation, Euler characteristic, surface classification, and Poincare-Hopf comparisons across the same surface.

Triangulation and cell decompositionEuler characteristicManifold

Hidden assumption

The source supports exactly this claim

Claim: A paper, textbook, caption, or course record justifies a sentence in the companion because the evidence supports that exact sentence.

Assumption: The source evidence actually supports the sentence being written, and the sentence does not silently borrow stronger authority from a nearby reference.

Why needed: Source discipline keeps plain language honest. A sentence can be simple and still overclaim if it treats a background paper, mirror listing, or auto-caption as stronger evidence than it is.

Plain check: Can the reader name the exact sentence, the source evidence behind it, and the stronger sentence that should remain unsaid?

Breaks if: It breaks if a recovered auto-caption is treated as exact lecture notes, or if a paper family is cited as proof that Tokieda stated a specific claim.

Course place: This assumption supports source readers, source nuance repairs, paper-family ledgers, and lecture source-faithfulness audits.

The Assumption Test

Use this page before applying a theorem or trusting a model. If an answer names only the result, ask which assumption gives that result permission to apply. If the assumption cannot be stated without formal language, return to the relevant concept page or theorem contract and rebuild the object from scratch.

A good explanation does not hide assumptions to feel simple. It states them in everyday language. The reader should know what would make the claim false: a changed boundary, a discontinuous jump, a missing defect, a bad sign convention, an incomplete state space, or an overstrong source sentence.

Repairing A Missing Assumption

When an answer skips an assumption, add the smallest sentence that makes the permission visible. For a deformation, say what was not allowed to move. For a count, say why the local events are ordinary and why signs share one rule. For a fixed-point claim, say why the rule maps the space back into itself. For a configuration space, say what one point records and what states have been removed.

The repair should not bury the reader in formal prerequisites. It should turn the prerequisite into a checkable sentence. The strongest repair has three parts: the claim being made, the condition that lets the claim speak, and the concrete failure that would make the claim stop applying.

Give no credit for an answer that says only that the theorem assumes the usual conditions. That phrase hides the work. Give credit when the answer names the particular condition in the current problem and shows how the reader could inspect it.

Why This Matters

Assumptions are not bureaucratic details. They are the bridge between a beautiful general statement and the object on the page. The course becomes mathematical when the reader can say why this strip, this route, this field, this surface, this model, or this source sentence is allowed to use the general idea.

This ledger should be used with the change ledger. The change ledger asks what changes and what survives. The assumption ledger asks why that change was legal in the first place. Together they prevent a page from sounding clear while quietly changing the problem.

When reviewing a page, mark the first sentence where a result is used. If the assumption is not visible nearby, the explanation is not ready yet.
