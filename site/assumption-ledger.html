Assumption Ledger

This page names the quiet conditions that let a mathematical sentence speak. A theorem, count, model, source claim, or deformation can carry an argument only after its assumptions have been checked in plain language.

How To Use The Ledger

Start with the claim that sounds convincing. Then ask what has to be true for that claim to be allowed. The assumption may be that the same object survived a deformation, that boundary data stayed fixed, that a rule stayed continuous, that all defects were counted, or that the source evidence supports exactly the sentence being written.

The plain check is the important part. It turns an abstract condition into something a reader can inspect: what moved, what stayed fixed, what was counted, what state was modeled, what source was used, and what stronger claim would break the account.

Hidden assumption

Same object through a deformation

Claim: A simplified picture answers the original question only when every simplifying move keeps the same rule-bearing object, so the easier final drawing is still evidence about the starting problem.

Assumption: Every step in the simplification keeps the rule-bearing parts under control: endpoints stay fixed when they define the route problem, gluing rules still identify the same points, obstacles remain forbidden, boundary behavior is preserved, and forbidden crossings do not sneak into the motion.

Why needed: Without this assumption, the proof may solve a cleaner but different problem. The course allows pictures to move only when the question survives the whole motion, not merely when the last picture looks easier to read.

Plain check: Can the reader point to the part that moved, name the part that was not allowed to change, and describe one in-between moment to show the forbidden event never happened?

Breaks if: It breaks if a route slides through an obstacle, a boundary endpoint moves freely, a glued edge is treated as an ordinary edge, or a crossing is erased by letting one curve pass through another.

Course place: This assumption runs through knot, route, quotient, and surface arguments where a messy drawing is replaced by a cleaner one. It is the check that turns redrawing into reasoning rather than decoration.

Hidden assumption

Boundary data is fixed

Claim: A crossing, route obstruction, or impossibility survives simplification only when the boundary information that defines the problem stayed fixed throughout the motion, so the final drawing still answers the original edge question.

Assumption: The boundary order, marked points, endpoint positions, side labels, or edge directions remain fixed while the inside of the picture changes. The edge data are not decoration; they are part of the problem statement.

Why needed: Many obstructions live in the boundary data. If the boundary can be rearranged, an apparent solution may only prove that a different boundary problem is easier, not that the original one was solved.

Plain check: Can the reader mark the data that live on the edge, say which of them must stay put, and explain why moving one of them would change the question being answered?

Breaks if: It breaks if endpoints are swapped, boundary labels are ignored, side directions are reversed without notice, or a path is allowed to leave the region it was supposed to stay inside.

Course place: This assumption appears when crossings are forced by endpoint order and when boundary orientation gives signs their shared reference. It teaches the reader to inspect the edge before trusting the interior picture.

Hidden assumption

Continuity has not been lost

Claim: A rule, path, deformation, or motion cannot jump past the obstruction only when every intermediate state is still accounted for, so the argument studies real gradual change rather than two disconnected snapshots.

Assumption: The change happens without sudden jumps: nearby inputs remain nearby outputs, and a moving system passes through the states between start and finish. The argument is about a continuous path or rule, not about choosing two pictures and ignoring the travel between them.

Why needed: Continuity is what lets shape force behavior. If jumps are allowed, a route can skip a wall, a fixed-point argument can avoid self-agreement, and a deformation can cross the forbidden event without paying attention to it.

Plain check: Can the reader describe what the intermediate states are, where they live, and why the motion cannot skip from before to after in one step without changing the proof's object?

Breaks if: It breaks if a map tears the space, a path jumps across a missing region, nearby inputs are sent to unrelated outputs, or a model lets the system pass through an impossible state.

Course place: This assumption supports deformation, fixed-point, and configuration-space arguments whenever a path or rule is used as evidence. It is the reason an obstruction cannot be escaped by skipping the in-between states.

Hidden assumption

The counted meetings are ordinary

Claim: A signed count of intersections can be used as evidence only after the meetings have been prepared so each one can be inspected locally, assigned a sign, and followed through small legal motion.

Assumption: The objects meet cleanly rather than through a fragile coincidence such as tangency, overlap, or several events happening at exactly the same place. Each meeting has a small neighborhood in which the local picture is readable.

Why needed: Signs and cancellations need meetings that can be inspected one at a time. Ordinary position makes the local event simple enough to count and stable enough that a small legal move does not create a mystery.

Plain check: Can the reader isolate one meeting, draw a small circle or window around it, assign its sign, and explain what nearby legal motion would do to that single event afterward?

Breaks if: It breaks if curves lie on top of each other, touch without crossing, or pass through a special accident that has not been separated into countable one-at-a-time events.

Course place: This assumption sits behind generic position, intersection number, and clean-meeting source families whenever crossings are counted. It explains why the course often cleans a picture before counting it carefully.

Hidden assumption

Signs have a shared direction rule

Claim: Plus and minus contributions can be added and trusted only when they come from one shared direction rule across the object, so cancellation means the same thing in every local place.

Assumption: The space, boundary, or local setup carries a consistent direction convention so that a plus sign in one place is comparable to a plus sign somewhere else. The reader must know where that convention comes from before adding signs.

Why needed: Signed arithmetic only works when the signs belong to the same rulebook. Without a shared direction rule, the symbols may look precise while adding unrelated local choices that cannot honestly cancel.

Plain check: Can the reader say where the direction convention comes from, carry it to the next meeting or defect, and notice whether the object blocks such a shared choice globally in practice?

Breaks if: It breaks if orientation is assumed on a surface that does not support it, if a boundary direction is changed halfway through, or if each crossing receives a sign from a private local choice.

Course place: This assumption supports boundary orientation, intersection number, turning counts, and index comparisons across one shared object. It is what makes plus and minus evidence comparable rather than decorative marks on drawings.

Hidden assumption

All defects are included

Claim: A total index or Poincare-Hopf comparison constrains the field only when all local defects belong to one complete account on the same surface, not a selected visible patch of it.

Assumption: Every isolated defect of the vector field on the relevant surface is counted, and the field satisfies the boundary or surface conditions required by the theorem. The word total means the whole list, not the visible favorites.

Why needed: A whole-surface theorem compares a complete local account with a whole-surface number. Leaving out one defect makes the local side describe a partial drawing while the right side still describes the whole surface.

Plain check: Can the reader name the surface, name the field, list every defect being counted, and state the surface number the total is compared with before using the theorem in the argument?

Breaks if: It breaks if a defect is hidden outside the drawing, if boundary behavior is ignored, if a non-isolated failure is counted as if it were isolated, or if the surface used for Euler characteristic is not the surface carrying the field.

Course place: This assumption is central to vector-field index and Poincare-Hopf explanations whenever local defects are added globally. It prevents a local count from pretending to be a whole-surface count.

Hidden assumption

One point records one full state

Claim: A configuration-space path represents a real physical motion only when each point records one complete allowed state, so movement in the model corresponds to movement the system can actually perform.

Assumption: Each point of the configuration space records all data needed for one complete allowed state, and every forbidden state has been removed before paths are studied. A point cannot stand for half a pose or hide a missing angle.

Why needed: A motion problem becomes topology only when the state space tells the truth about the system. Missing freedoms or missing obstacles make paths in the model promise motions the physical system cannot perform.

Plain check: Can the reader say what one point records, what has been removed, and how a continuous path through such points translates back into the original moving system step by step?

Breaks if: It breaks if collisions are left inside the state space, if a necessary angle or position is not recorded, if forbidden states are removed only from the picture and not from the model, or if two different physical states are accidentally treated as one.

Course place: This assumption supports configuration-space and product-space modeling in the later motion examples. It is the check that keeps a state-space drawing tied to real possible motion rather than a sketch.

Hidden assumption

The rule maps the space to itself

Claim: A fixed-point theorem or graph-and-diagonal argument can be used only when the rule stays inside one space, so self-agreement compares a point with its own allowed output.

Assumption: The rule takes every allowed input point back into the same space, and the continuity and boundary conditions required by the argument remain true. The input room and output room are the same room for the purpose of the theorem.

Why needed: A fixed point is self-agreement inside one space. If the rule sends some outputs outside, changes the boundary setup, or jumps, the diagonal comparison no longer asks whether a point returns to itself in the original space.

Plain check: Can the reader name the space, name the rule, test a typical output, and explain why every output is still an allowed point of that same space after the rule acts?

Breaks if: It breaks if outputs land outside the region, if the rule jumps, if the boundary behavior used by the theorem has been changed, or if the domain and target quietly become different spaces.

Course place: This assumption supports fixed-point pages, Brouwer contracts, and graph-and-diagonal explanations where self-agreement is forced. It is the check before any existence claim is allowed to speak.

Hidden assumption

A cell count describes the same surface

Claim: Euler characteristic survives changing the cell decomposition only when the new cell account still describes the same completed surface, with the same boundary behavior and gluing rules in force throughout.

Assumption: The vertices, edges, and faces form a valid account of the same surface before and after the cutting or refinement is changed. The new bookkeeping may be cleaner or busier, but it must cover the same completed surface.

Why needed: Euler characteristic is a whole-surface ledger, not a reward for one drawing. The count survives because artificial internal bookkeeping changes cancel while the surface itself remains the same object.

Plain check: Can the reader name the surface being counted, describe what changed in the cell account, and explain why the new cells still cover that surface with the same boundary and gluing behavior?

Breaks if: It breaks if a boundary is lost, a glued edge is counted twice incorrectly, a face is counted before identifications are applied, or the drawing is changed into a different surface while keeping the old label.

Course place: This assumption supports triangulation, Euler characteristic, surface classification, and Poincare-Hopf comparisons across the same surface. It keeps arithmetic attached to the object rather than to a convenient sketch alone.

Hidden assumption

The source supports exactly this claim

Claim: A paper, textbook, caption, or course record justifies a sentence in the companion only when the evidence supports that exact sentence, not a stronger nearby interpretation or broader course claim.

Assumption: The source evidence actually supports the sentence being written, and the sentence does not silently borrow stronger authority from a nearby reference, a broader paper family, or a remembered course theme.

Why needed: Source discipline keeps plain language honest. A sentence can be simple and still overclaim if it treats a background paper, mirror listing, recovered caption, or auto-caption as stronger evidence than it is.

Plain check: Can the reader name the exact sentence, the source evidence behind it, the kind of support that evidence provides, and the stronger sentence that remains unsupported by that evidence trail?

Breaks if: It breaks if a recovered auto-caption is treated as exact lecture notes, if a paper family is cited as proof that Tokieda stated a specific claim, or if a helpful interpretation is presented as transcript fact.

Course place: This assumption supports source readers, source nuance repairs, paper-family ledgers, and lecture source-faithfulness audits. It keeps the companion's confidence matched to the evidence trail for each page-specific sentence.

The Assumption Test

Use this page before applying a theorem or trusting a model. If an answer names only the result, ask which assumption gives that result permission to apply. If the assumption cannot be stated without formal language, return to the relevant concept page or theorem contract and rebuild the object from scratch.

A plain explanation does not hide assumptions to feel simple. It states them in everyday language. The failure condition has to be visible: a changed boundary, a discontinuous jump, a missing defect, a bad sign convention, an incomplete state space, or an overstrong source sentence.

Repairing A Missing Assumption

When an answer skips an assumption, add the smallest sentence that makes the permission visible. For a deformation, say what was not allowed to move. For a count, say why the local events are ordinary and why signs share one rule. For a fixed-point claim, say why the rule maps the space back into itself. For a configuration space, say what one point records and what states have been removed.

The repair should not bury the reader in formal prerequisites. It should turn the prerequisite into a checkable sentence. The strongest repair has three parts: the claim being made, the condition that lets the claim speak, and the concrete failure that would make the claim stop applying.

Give no credit for an answer that says only that the theorem assumes the usual conditions. That phrase hides the work. Give credit when the answer names the particular condition in the current problem and shows how the reader could inspect it.

Why This Matters

Assumptions are not bureaucratic details. They are the bridge between a beautiful general statement and the object on the page. The course becomes mathematical when the reader can say why this strip, this route, this field, this surface, this model, or this source sentence is allowed to use the general idea.

This ledger should be used with the change ledger. The change ledger asks what changes and what survives. The assumption ledger asks why that change was legal in the first place. Together they prevent a page from sounding clear while quietly changing the problem.

When reviewing a page, mark the first sentence where a result is used. If the assumption is not visible nearby, the explanation is not ready yet.
