Concept Dependencies

This page is a reading order for the course ideas. It shows which earlier concepts carry the load for later theorem-level ideas, and what question proves that the dependency has been understood.

How To Use This Map

Do not read the course as a flat list of terms. Later ideas rely on earlier agreements: what counts as the same space, which motions are legal, why signs mean anything, and how local data can add to a whole-surface demand. If a later theorem feels sudden, walk backward through the dependency row that supports it.

The right test is not whether a name is familiar. The test is whether the reader can say what the earlier idea lets the later idea do. A quotient lets edge labels become a surface. Orientation lets signs become evidence. Euler characteristic lets Poincare-Hopf speak about motion.

From local patch evidence to whole-surface behavior

A small patch can look ordinary while the whole surface changes what routes and sides mean.

Understand first: Topology and geometryBoundary and orientation

Then read: ManifoldQuotient space

Why this dependency matters: This is the first dependency in the course. Before a reader can trust later surface arguments, they need to see that local evidence is not enough. Quotient rules and manifold language give that local-versus-global gap a precise form.

Reader check: Can I say what is locally ordinary, and what whole-surface fact is still undecided?

From legal motion to protected mathematical evidence in proof

A picture may move, but the proof needs a fact that moves with it unchanged.

Understand first: DeformationGeneric position

Then read: InvariantParity

Why this dependency matters: Deformation by itself is only motion. Generic position cleans the picture enough to inspect it. Invariant and parity add evidence: something survives the motion and can rule out a desired ending.

Reader check: What exact move is legal, and what fact is still true after that move?

From built spaces to durable surface classification rules

A space built from choices, identifications, or replacements can be sorted by durable surface features.

Understand first: Product spaceQuotient spaceSurgery

Then read: ManifoldBoundary and orientationEuler characteristic

Why this dependency matters: Products make choice-spaces, quotients make sameness rules, and surgery changes a surface under boundary control. Those operations prepare the reader to count faces, track orientation, and name the surface by what survives simplification.

Reader check: What construction rule made this space, and which surface features did that rule create or preserve?

From surface ledger to stable whole-shape number

A surface count first looks like bookkeeping, then becomes a number that can control geometry and motion.

Understand first: Triangulation and cell decompositionEuler characteristic

Then read: Gauss-Bonnet as total turningPoincare-Hopf theorem

Why this dependency matters: Triangulation makes a soft surface finite enough to count. Euler characteristic shows that the final account does not belong to one mesh. Later, Gauss-Bonnet and Poincare-Hopf use that same whole-surface number to constrain turning and vector-field defects.

Reader check: Is this number attached to one drawing, or to the surface after redrawings and refinements?

From clean meetings to signed obstruction under deformation

Clean meetings can be assigned signs, and the signed total can force a later meeting to exist.

Understand first: Generic positionBoundary and orientationWinding and linking

Then read: Intersection numberFixed points

Why this dependency matters: Generic position separates meetings. Orientation gives signs meaning. Winding and linking train the idea of a stable relation. Intersection number combines these into a count that survives legal motion, then fixed-point theory uses meetings between graph and diagonal.

Reader check: Are the meetings clean, and do the signs come from a real direction rule?

From a rule to forced self-agreement inside space

A rule becomes a shape in a larger space; self-agreement becomes a meeting with the diagonal.

Understand first: Product spaceDual picturesIntersection number

Then read: Fixed pointsBrouwer fixed-point theorem

Why this dependency matters: Product space holds input and output together. Duality allows the rule to be redrawn as a graph. Intersection number explains why a graph may be unable to avoid the diagonal. Brouwer is the closed-ball version where the space itself blocks total escape.

Reader check: What is the graph, what is the diagonal, and why would their meeting mean a fixed point?

From arrow-field failure to signed index around defects

A place where motion stops can be read by how nearby arrows turn around it.

Understand first: Fixed pointsEquilibriumGeneric position

Then read: Vector field indexPoincare-Hopf theorem

Why this dependency matters: Fixed points teach forced special states. Equilibrium turns that idea into a failure of an arrow field. Generic position keeps failures isolated. Vector-field index makes each failure countable, and Poincare-Hopf adds those counts over the whole surface.

Reader check: Is the defect isolated, and what do the arrows do on a small loop around it?

From state-space model to physical application constraints

A physical problem becomes usable only after its possible states are turned into the right mathematical space.

Understand first: Configuration spaceFixed pointsPoincare-Hopf theorem

Then read: Dual picturesInvariantVector field index

Why this dependency matters: Configuration space lists the possible states. Fixed points and Poincare-Hopf supply forced-behavior tools. Duality and invariants keep the translation honest by asking whether the new picture still means the original physical question.

Reader check: Does the state space include the real freedoms and exclude the real forbidden states?
