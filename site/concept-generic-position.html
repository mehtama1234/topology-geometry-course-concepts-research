Generic position

Generic position exists because special coincidences are too brittle to reason from. If three things meet at exactly one point, or a curve just kisses another curve, a tiny nudge can change the picture. The course wants arguments that survive tiny nudges, so it first moves the picture away from accidental perfection.

Concept Essay

Start with a sheet of paper and a pen. Draw two curves that touch at exactly one point—they kiss. Now nudge the paper sideways; most of the time, those two curves no longer touch at all. They have separated. That delicate kiss was a coincidence; it depended on getting the angle, speed, and position exactly right. The same fragility haunts more complex setups: three lines meeting at a single dot, an object balanced along an entire edge instead of at one isolated spot, or two surfaces that meet along a perfect seam. These configurations are delicate. A tiny motion can scatter them into separate, isolated events.

Generic position is the course's refusal to build a proof on such delicate scaffolding. If you are counting how many times two curves cross, you do not want those crossings to pile up in a heap at one spot—then you cannot separate them and count each one. If you are assigning a sign (plus or minus) to each intersection, you need the intersection to have a clear local picture, not a smeared tangle where you cannot see what is going on. If your argument relies on finding isolated defects or critical points, an equilibrium spread across an entire wire gives you an endless mess instead of points you can name and track. Generic position moves the entire picture just enough to shatter these accidents into readable, individual, countable events.

Here is the key insight: how do you know such a move is allowed? The answer is that almost every small movement works. If you have a pencil line on paper and you nudge it by a random tiny amount, the probability that you land on another accident is zero—accidents are rare and thin and special. This is why generic position has real force. It says: your question rests on stable facts that survive typical small changes. After you move away from accidents, the picture you see is not a rare gimmick; it is the normal, ordinary case. That ordinary case is more honest because it shows the mechanism that persists under small motion, not just a fragile geometry that one wrong breath could collapse.

Tokieda's arguments in this course lean on this promise over and over. When counting pairs of intersections that are born and die, you need the events to happen one at a time, not all at once in a chaos. When checking that a surface has a certain property, you work with a configuration where each piece sits in a clear local position, not crushed against a boundary or tangled with others. When tracking how a curve winds around a point, you do not want the curve to pass through the point itself—that would make the whole picture collapse. Generic position is the permission slip that lets you assume all these things without loss of generality. The phrase without loss of generality is the core: you are not ignoring special cases; you are saying that special cases emerge as limits or transitions from the stable picture you studied first.

The tempting mistake is to think that the exact, accidental arrangement is more honest or revealing. A curve that kisses another curve does look precise; three lines meeting at a dot does look neat and defined. But that precision is fragile. The real question is not whether you can draw it—you can, with a steady hand. The real question is whether the geometry survives a small change. If it does not, then what you were relying on was an accident, not a structure. Accidents do not tell the proof anything; structures do. A clean crossing tells you something true about the shapes. A whole line of equilibria tells you almost nothing until you perturb it and see what happens.

To make this concrete: suppose you are studying how a curve on a sphere behaves as you change one parameter. The curve sweeps out a surface. At a generic configuration, the curve crosses itself at isolated points, and each crossing has a clear sign. But at a special parameter value, the curve might touch itself along an entire arc. That arc is a boundary—it shows where something transitions from one state to another. To understand the full story, you study the generic configurations on either side of that boundary, then take limits to see how the boundary sits between them. The generic configurations are not easier to read because they skip details; they are easier to read because they let you inspect each event separately. The rare, boundary configurations show up as singular limits of those ordinary generic pictures. In this way, generic position does not deny the special cases; it puts them in their right place as exceptional transitions between stable regimes.

This thinking applies far beyond topology whenever you want to extract a dependable answer from a messy situation. In an experiment, exact alignment between two measurements is almost never a signal—it is noise or coincidence. A physicist moves the apparatus slightly and asks if the answer changes. If it does not, the answer is dependable. If it does, something fragile and accidental was propping up the old answer. In computer simulations, when several events happen at nearly the same time, you cannot tell the order or causal relationship. A small change to the parameters spreads those events out in time, and then the order becomes clear. The principle is the same: start from a configuration where accidents have been removed, read what persists, and then understand special cases as limits or transitions from that picture. This is how you separate the real structure from the accidents.

First-Principles Long Essay

Ordinary problem: Imagine you are examining a piece of machinery where a rod touches a surface at exactly one spot, and from that one spot, a crucial measurement is supposed to follow. The problem is, that spot is so delicate that if the rod moves even a millimeter or the surface settles a tiny bit, the contact point shifts entirely. You cannot trust a conclusion built on something so fragile. So you adjust things: move the rod or the surface slightly until the contact becomes a clean, stable crossing or a clear separation. Now when the rod or surface shifts a little—as real objects always do in the world—the crossing or separation survives. You can count on what you have measured because it does not depend on exact alignment. This is the everyday problem: look at a situation where coincidences and accidents are muddying the picture, and ask whether the key conclusions would change if things were slightly different. If they do change, your conclusions are built on sand. If they do not change, you have found something real about the structure. Generic position is the habit of stepping back, making a small adjustment to remove the coincidences, and then inspecting the stable, dependable picture that remains.

First Principles

Imagine two pencil lines drawn on a piece of rubber. You can draw them so they cross at a corner where four floor tiles meet. But stretch the rubber a little, and that perfect corner alignment vanishes—the crossing slides into the middle of one tile, and the accident is gone. Generic position means shifting or tweaking your setup just enough to destroy these accidental alignments. Once the accident is gone, intersections happen one at a time, contacts are crisp, and you can count what is actually there without coincidences hiding the real structure.

Important Detail

The adjustment must be small enough that the object itself does not change shape in any way that matters to your question. You are removing coincidences, not altering the rubber, the bending rules, or the problem you are trying to solve. The moved-around picture still answers your original question—it just does so in a clearer way.

A fact that survives small, controlled disturbances is a fact you can trust.
