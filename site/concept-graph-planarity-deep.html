Graph planarity — when a network cannot avoid crossing itself

Imagine you have five telephone hubs and you must connect every pair. On a single-layer circuit board, can you wire them so no two wires cross? Sometimes yes, sometimes no — and the reason has nothing to do with cleverness. Euler's counting law, V − E + F = 2, turns the crossing question into arithmetic: if the crossing-free layout must satisfy a certain balance of nodes and edges, but your network violates it, then a crossing is unavoidable. Here is that idea, a real run that proves one famous graph cannot be drawn flat, why the counting settles it, what it means for real circuit boards, and the limits of the argument.

1 · The problem — some wiring just will not fit on one layer

You have five telephone hubs arranged somewhere, and you need to wire every pair together so they can all talk. On a flat circuit board with no vertical layers, you must draw five dots and ten lines (one for each pair), all on the same flat surface. Try it. You will find that no matter how you arrange the five dots, at least two lines must cross. But try the same game with four hubs, or six hubs with only some pairs connected, and suddenly it works. The question is: how do you know in advance whether a network can fit flat without any crossings, or whether crossing is mathematically forced?

2 · The reframe — translate crossing into arithmetic

The leap: if your network lives on a flat sheet, it divides the sheet into regions — think of it like a map with countries. Every country has a boundary made of wire. Now use the same trick from Euler's ancient discovery about polyhedra: count the nodes (V), wires or edges (E), and regions or faces (F), and take the combination V − E + F. If your network is drawn flat and connected, this combination always equals 2. That is a hard law of flat surfaces. So now the question flips: if your network's node and edge count makes it mathematically impossible for any flat layout to have an integer number of faces, then no flat layout exists.

The hidden quantity is whether the graph hides a forbidden pattern — a complete five-vertex graph or the three-houses-three-utilities graph; its presence is exactly what makes a flat, crossing-free drawing impossible.

3 · Seen on a real run — K5 cannot be drawn without crossing

We took the complete network K5 — five nodes, every pair connected, so ten edges total — and asked: can Euler's formula force this to cross? The counting.

If K5 can be drawn flat, then V − E + F = 2 with V = 5 and E = 10, so F = 2 − 5 + 10 = 7 faces. But The catch: every face is surrounded by at least three edges (the shortest boundary is a triangle). And every edge borders exactly two faces. So the total count of edge-face meetings is at least 3 times 7, and it is at most 2 times 10. That means 3F ≤ 2E, or E ≥ 1.5F. For seven faces, that needs at least 10.5 edges. But K5 has only 10 edges.

The discovered fact: K5 simply cannot be drawn flat. The mathematics of counting nodes, edges, and regions makes it impossible. No amount of rearranging the five nodes on paper will ever let you connect every pair without a crossing. The same argument proves that another graph, K3,3 (three water hubs and three house hubs, each water hub connected to all three houses) also cannot avoid a crossing. These two graphs are the smallest 'witnesses' to the impossibility — they are the simplest networks that refuse to fit flat.

4 · Why the counting settles it

The reason is that any flat drawing is forced into Euler's balance V − E + F = 2. That balance comes from the geometry of the flat plane itself — it is not a choice, it is a law. Now, every face (every region) must have a boundary, and the shortest boundary is three edges (a triangle). So no region can exist unless it has at least three edges around it. Similarly, every edge sits on the boundary of exactly two regions, because it divides the flat plane into an inside and an outside. These facts together mean that the total number of 'region-edge touches' is between 3F (if every region is a triangle) and 2E (the true count). So 3F ≤ 2E. Rearrange: E ≥ 1.5F.

For K5, Euler forces F = 7, so we need E ≥ 10.5. But K5 has only 10 edges. The network's own structure — five nodes, all pairs wired — sets E at exactly 10. Since 10 is less than 10.5, no flat layout is possible. The counting does not just suggest a crossing; it forbids any configuration without one.

5 · The one limit — the test works for the famous culprits, but not all graphs

The argument above catches K5 and K3,3 — the two smallest non-planar networks — and it gives a fast yes-or-no for many simple cases. But the full answer to 'can an arbitrary graph fit flat?' is more subtle. Kuratowski's theorem says a graph is planar if and only if it contains no copy of K5 or K3,3 (even hidden inside a tangle of other nodes). Finding that hidden copy is a computationally harder job than just checking the edge and face bounds. For practical circuit boards, the inequality E ≤ 1.5V − 3 (which follows from our counting argument for planar graphs) is a fast filter, but it will not definitively prove that every graph satisfying the bound is drawable flat.

Also, this whole argument assumes you are drawing on a flat plane. On a torus (a surface with a hole), or a higher-dimensional space, different networks may fit. The planarity jail is specific to two dimensions.

6 · The surprise — this is why multi-layer circuit boards exist

The real shock is that this ancient Greek counting formula — vertices minus edges plus faces equals two — built for polyhedra in the 1700s, sits at the heart of a practical industrial problem. When engineers design a circuit board and the connections get too tangled, they hit a Euler limit. K5 cannot be drawn flat, period. So the board needs a second copper layer, or a third. The routing algorithms that allocate wires to layers are actually solving this graph-planarity problem. Each layer can hold a planar subgraph; adding layers gives more nodes and edges to work with.

Chip designers know that some routing problems require multiple layers because the network topology — the pure connectivity, stripped of all geometry — forbids a flat solution. They are not fighting physics or engineering limits; they are butting against mathematics. The same Euler characteristic that indexes holes in a torus, that counts the curvature on a bent surface, and that forces stagnation points on a spinning sphere also dictates where you can draw a wire without a crossing. It is the same number in disguise, woven into the structure of every connected thing.

7 · Where it is used in practice — Determining if a network can be drawn without crossings

Circuit board routing decides how many copper layers are needed: K5 and K3,3 graphs are provably impossible to route on a single layer, forcing multi-layer designs. Chip design algorithms use planarity testing as a fast filter: if a net is non-planar, it requires a layer-switching strategy. Infrastructure design (water, electricity, telecommunications) tests if networks can coexist without interference by checking if their graphs are planar on a shared physical space.

Planarity acts as a hard constraint on the topology of connections. A planar subgraph can be embedded in 2D without crossings; if your network requires more than a planar graph, it must span higher dimensions or layers. In circuit routing, each layer is a planar embedding; non-planar nets demand multiple layers. In infrastructure, interference is avoided if and only if the network graph remains planar in the physical layout.

Graph planarity is an impossibility proof: certain networks cannot fit flat, regardless of how cleverly you rearrange nodes.
