Planar graphs

Planarity exists because connection problems are also space problems. A graph may only say which dots must be joined, but drawing it on a plane adds a constraint: the routes must share a surface without forbidden crossings.

Concept Essay

Imagine drawing a road map. You have a set of cities that must be connected by roads, and you want no road to cross another unnecessarily—perhaps because intersections cost money or create hazards. You might draw the roads on paper and find they keep crossing no matter how you rearrange the cities. The question is simple: does the requirement to connect these cities force crossings, or is your layout just clumsy? Planar graphs answers this by separating the demand (which cities must connect) from the room available (what a flat surface supplies for routing).

Start with a concrete example. Draw five dots and connect each one to all four others—every pair must be directly linked. This is called the complete graph on five vertices. Try it. No matter how you shuffle the dots around or curve the lines, you cannot avoid crossings. This is not a failure of imagination; it's a fact about how flat surfaces work. The surface has run out of routes. On a torus (a donut shape), you could draw the same five cities without any crossing, because a torus has a different structure—paths can slip around each other through the hole. This shows that planarity depends on the surface you're working with, not just the graph itself.

The wrong intuition is that a graph is either drawable or not, period. The truth is more subtle: a graph is drawable on a specific surface without crossings. Some graphs fit on a plane; others require a surface with holes or handles. The mathematician's job is to determine which surfaces work for which graphs and to prove it rigorously. The proof cannot rely on your drawing ability. It must show that the very structure of the connection requirement and the surface constraints force the outcome, every time.

Here's how the separation works in practice. The graph tells you what must be connected. The surface tells you what routes are available and how they interact. On a plane, two paths that start at different points must either meet or stay apart—there's no third option like a bridge. On a surface with holes, paths can navigate around obstacles in new ways. The mathematics tracks how this changes. For instance, Euler's formula relates the number of vertices, edges, and faces in a planar graph: V − E + F = 2 (where V is vertices, E is edges, F is faces). This formula works exactly because a plane has no holes, and it fails in different ways on surfaces with handles or holes.

The power of this idea lies in turning a drawing problem into a counting problem. Instead of trying millions of arrangements, you ask: does the connection structure match the surface's capacity? An edge can be drawn, a face can be bounded, and the counting formula forces a conclusion. If a graph violates the counting constraints, no arrangement will ever work—not because you lack artistic skill, but because the mathematics forbids it. This is how topology proves impossibility without checking every drawing.

Planar graphs also trains your intuition for a pattern that recurs throughout geometry and topology. When you have a space (a surface, a manifold, a network) and you want to place things in it (curves, subspaces, connections), the question shifts from 'can I draw it' to 'does the space have room for all these requirements at once?' This appears in configuration spaces (can all the robots move simultaneously without collision?), in knot theory (can this tangle be untangled on a sphere versus a torus?), and in intersection problems (can these surfaces meet without crossing in a higher-dimensional space?). Planarity is the simplest example of this universal principle.

Beyond the classroom, planarity matters in circuit design, transport networks, and utility routing. Engineers need to know whether a circuit can be printed on a single layer without rewiring, or whether pipes and roads can coexist in a shared space. The same topological question—do the required connections fit in the available surface or layers?—powers the real problem. Topology contributes the shape reasoning; the engineer supplies the practical costs and constraints. But without the topological separation of demand from room, the engineer would be stuck guessing or brute-forcing layouts. Planar graphs gives a framework for thinking about what's possible and what's not.

The real depth emerges when you ask why topology cares. A graph itself is abstract—it says nothing about whether lines curve or fold or wrap around. Topology extracts what matters: the fact that on a plane, two paths cannot pass through each other unless they are allowed to cross. That is a structural fact about the plane itself, not about how well you draw. When you move to different surfaces—a sphere, a torus, a projective plane—the structural facts change. A complete graph on five vertices that cannot be drawn on a plane might be drawable on a torus. This reveals the deep idea: planarity is not a property of the graph alone. It is a relationship between the graph and the surface. The graph is drawable or not depending on whether the surface has enough navigational freedom. This distinction changes how you think about the problem from a puzzle into a classification task.

First Principles

A graph is dots and the lines connecting them. The real question is whether you can draw those connections on a flat surface without the lines crossing. But here's the catch: a failed drawing might just mean you arranged things poorly, not that it's impossible. Planarity asks which graphs can survive any rearrangement without crossings—it separates what the connection demand forces from what your available surface permits.

Important Detail

A single unsuccessful drawing proves nothing. You must show that every conceivable arrangement, no matter how cleverly you reroute or reposition vertices, still runs into unavoidable crossings. That's when you know the surface itself is the obstacle, not your drawing skill.

Principle Behind It

If a surface can hold all required connections without any crossing, the graph is planar on that surface. Whether this works depends on both the number of connections demanded and the space available to route them. A topological argument shows that certain connection patterns force crossings regardless of repositioning.

Failure Mode

A failed drawing proves only that the drawer failed. A topological argument proves that every drawing must fail because the surface itself lacks the required room. The evidence has to come from endpoint order, separation, parity, or another protected fact that survives redrawing, not from one crowded sketch.
