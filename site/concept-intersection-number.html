Intersection number

Intersection number exists because visible crossings alone are unstable. During a legal motion, crossings can be born or die in pairs. The signed count remembers whether the meetings carry a net obstruction after those pair changes cancel.

Concept Essay

Imagine two chains lying on a table, tangled and crossing each other at several points. You want to count how many times they touch. If you just count the visible crossings, you get a raw number—say, five. But now slide one chain slightly without breaking it or lifting it off the table. Maybe two crossings fuse into one, or one crossing vanishes, or a new crossing is born somewhere else. Your count jumps to three, or seven, or something else. What felt like a fact about the chains turns out to be an accident of how you drew them. The raw count does not tell you which meetings are essential, baked into the geometry, and which are accidents of the drawing—artifacts of your choice about where to place the chains on the page.

This is the core problem: not all crossings are created equal. Some crossings can be removed by a gentle motion without breaking the rules of the game. Others cannot be removed—they represent a real, forced meeting that geometry demands. A raw count has no way to tell them apart. You need a number that captures only the meetings that carry information, the ones that survive any legal rearrangement. The challenge is to extract from a picture something solid enough to trust. Without such a tool, every time you redraw the picture to make it cleaner, your count changes, and you cannot tell if the change means something about the objects themselves or just about your drawing skill.

The solution is to add orientation: give each chain a direction, like an arrow pointing along it. At each crossing point, look at the two arrows and ask a simple question: do they turn the same way around the point, or opposite? If they turn the same way, mark it plus. If they turn opposite ways, mark it minus. Now here is the surprise: when a legal motion creates two new crossings (in opposite signs), they cancel each other. When a legal motion destroys two opposite crossings, they vanish as a pair. The total sum stays constant no matter what motion you perform.

Why does this work? Because orientation is rigid and geometric. Once you decide which way the arrow points along the chain, it does not change just because you wiggled the curve in space. The sign rule is built into the geometry itself—into how the curves are positioned and oriented to each other. When a deformation brings two crossings into existence, their signs are forced to be opposite by the orientation rule that is already in place. They are born as a plus-minus pair, and together they contribute zero to the total. This is not an accident or a gimmick; it is pure geometry at work, a consequence of how orientation forces signs to appear in pairs.

Tokieda shows this by hand on paper: two pieces of string, each with an arrow drawn on it, crossing multiple times at different points. Erase the curves and redraw them slightly using only legal motions. The total of all the signed crossings does not change. New crossings may appear, old ones may vanish, but they appear and vanish in canceling pairs. Watch it happen: a small wiggle brings two new crossings (one plus, one minus), and they add nothing to the total. Another motion erases two opposite crossings, and the total stays the same. The signed count records something deeper than any single drawing: it records an obstruction that no legal motion can wash away. This obstruction is the intersection number.

The concept teaches why signed counts beat raw counts in topology and geometry. It is the reason that later chapters can treat fixed points as intersections of a graph with a diagonal line, and why vector fields can be counted by their index around a singularity. In each case, orientation and clean position establish the signs, and those signs are trustworthy because they survive any legal deformation. The power comes from the fact that orientation is a global choice, not a local one. A picture becomes a number not because we forgot the geometry, but because the geometry itself—the orientation, the position, the way the curves meet—is what gives the signs their meaning and force. The number carries geometry inside it.

This is also where the limits of the concept become clear. Intersection number only works if two conditions hold: the curves must meet transversely, meaning they cross cleanly without touching tangentially, and orientation must be defined everywhere on both curves. If curves are tangent to each other, they touch without crossing, and the sign becomes unclear because the orientation rule does not apply to a tangent meeting. If one object has no well-defined orientation—if you cannot put a consistent arrow all the way around it—then the count may not exist or may not survive deformation. The concept teaches discipline: count only the evidence that geometry protects.

In the end, intersection number answers a precise question: if two objects move around within the allowed limits, what about their meetings can we remember for certain? The answer is the signed count—not every crossing visible in one drawing, but every crossing weighted by orientation, and only when orientation is consistent everywhere and the meeting is clean. That signed count is what carries meaning from one drawing to the next, surviving motion and deformation as a genuine invariant of the geometry. It is a bridge between the picture and arithmetic, and the bridge is built from orientation, not arbitrary choice. Once you understand why the signs have to appear in opposite pairs during deformation, you see why intersection number is worth trusting.

First Principles

Intersection number counts where two curves cross, but with a twist: each crossing gets plus or minus depending on how the curves are oriented. A raw count of crossings is useless because a small motion can suddenly create or destroy two opposite crossings. The signed count is designed so that when opposite meetings appear together, they cancel out, leaving only the meetings that cannot be erased by legal motion.

Important Detail

The signs must come from a consistent orientation rule—a direction or handedness rule that applies everywhere. Without orientation, plus and minus are arbitrary, and the count becomes meaningless. The sign discipline is what makes the total survive motion.

Principle Behind It

When two oriented curves meet transversely at a point, assign plus if their orientation vectors turn the same way around the crossing point, minus if opposite. The sum of all signs is the intersection number. This total is invariant under deformation that keeps the curves transverse.

Failure Mode

Counting crossings before explaining signs loses the evidence. The sign records how oriented pieces meet inside the surrounding space. Without that direction information, cancellation may be wishful arithmetic rather than a mathematical reason. The count must say which meetings are real evidence and which appear only as canceling pairs.
