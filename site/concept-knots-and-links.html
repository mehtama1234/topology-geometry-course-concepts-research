Knots and links

Knots and links exist in the course because a curve in space can remember how it is embedded. Length, roundness, and exact position can change, yet the loop may still be unable to become a plain circle by legal motion.

Concept Essay

Grab a piece of string and tie it loosely into a knot by hand. Now drop the two loose ends together and pinch them—you have made a closed loop, a knot that lives in three-dimensional space. The question Tokieda asks is immediate: can you wiggle and reshape that loop until it becomes an unknotted circle? You may stretch it, compress it, slide any part over any other part. The one rule is that the string cannot pass through itself. Most tangled messes yield to patient working. But some loops refuse to untangle no matter how long you try. That refusal is not evidence of your skill or patience. It is evidence that the loop is locked into a shape that memory protects.

The difference between a flat drawing and a three-dimensional loop matters desperately. Draw a knot on paper with a pen, showing where one strand crosses over or under another. That drawing is only a map—a projection of a real rope onto a page. The crossing points hide the critical information: which strand sits above? The ink looks the same if the strand is underneath. Change the over-under order at a single crossing, and you may transform a knot into an unknot or vice versa, yet the drawing's lines stay in the same place. This is why Tokieda stresses recording the layers. The physical loop tells you what layer is what. The flat drawing needs labels or explicit marks to keep that memory alive.

To see the force of the no-passing rule, imagine a world where string could phase through itself like a ghost. In that world, every tangled knot could be unknotted by pulling it through itself. The rule against passing through is what transforms a knot from visual clutter into a topological lock. When you forbid the pass-through, the path the curve takes through space becomes locked in place. The loop has no thickness in the mathematical model—it is a purely one-dimensional object—yet the prohibition gives it memory. It remembers its route even though it occupies no volume. This is the core insight: constraints create structure. Remove the constraint, and the structure vanishes.

Tokieda teaches this constraint through physical strips. Take a Möbius strip—a paper loop with a half-twist—and cut along its length off-center, not down the middle. The two pieces remain linked, attached to each other like two links of a chain, even though you never cut all the way through and separated them by distance. They cannot come free without tearing the paper. This linking survives reshaping and bending the paper strips. It is invisible in any still image alone but becomes visible when you trace your finger along the boundary and see that neither piece can escape the other. The strips teach the same principle as knots: spatial routing creates locks that persist.

The space around the loop is part of the knot itself. A rope tied in a circle and left on a table can be untangled by someone with access to a third dimension—someone can lift one strand over another in the air. But a knot confined to a flat plane cannot always be untangled using only planar motions. The knot is not just the curve; it is the curve plus the surrounding room it occupies plus the motions that are permitted. Change the available space or the allowed moves, and you change which knots are equivalent to which. This is why the same tangle might be untangleable in three dimensions but trapped in two, or trapped in three dimensions but free in four.

Asking whether a knot is truly knotted means asking whether it is impossible to remove the tangle under the legal rules. The tools that answer this question are invariants—properties of the knot that do not change when you apply legal motions. A knot might have a crossing number, a winding number, or a more abstract algebraic invariant. If one loop has an invariant that another loop cannot acquire by legal reshaping, then they must be topologically different. No sequence of legal moves can transform one into the other. These invariants are not the knot itself; they are fingerprints left by the knot's structure. A single invariant is like one clue in a detective story—it may prove the loops are different, but it does not fully describe the loop's essence.

Why does the course include knots and links? Because they isolate the discipline of legal motion to its sharpest form. Whenever Tokieda asks what property survives deformation, he is asking whether some invariant remains. Knots teach that question in its clearest case: given a curve and a set of rules about which moves are allowed, which properties of the curve are forced to stay constant? This same reasoning appears later when the course considers submanifolds bending and twisting through higher-dimensional spaces, when it counts intersections that cannot be erased by moving the surfaces, or when it models configurations of a mechanical system that cannot reach each other without violating a constraint. The discipline is always the same: distinguish the appearance from the structure, define the legal moves precisely, find what survives, and use that remnant as evidence.

Ordinary problem: Imagine you are winding a thick electrical cord or a garden hose, or tying up a dock line on a boat. You bundle it, coil it, drape it, and wind it in ways that seem compact and orderly. Days or weeks later, someone tries to pull the loose end, expecting the cord to unwind smoothly, but it resists. The loose end gets pulled tight against a knot. Now you face a practical puzzle: can you undo this tangle by hand without cutting the cord and without somehow passing the cord through itself? In some cases, the answer is clearly yes—the knot is just carelessly wound, and patient wiggling sets it free. In other cases, no amount of careful reshaping works. The knot seems to have a stubborn structure, as if the cord has locked itself into a configuration that resists all legal untangling. This everyday frustration points to a deeper question: what is the difference between a tangle that only looks bad and a tangle that is structurally locked? What remains unchanged when you reshape and wiggle everything you are allowed to reshape? That question defines the need for knot theory: not to memorize names of knots, but to develop a language for distinguishing genuine structural locks from mere disorder.

First Principles

A knot is a closed loop of rope or cord that cannot become an ordinary circle by bending and sliding alone, without cutting or passing through itself. The deep question is not whether it looks messy on paper, but whether certain loop configurations carry structural memory that survives all legal motion. This turns the problem into a precise rule: what stays locked when everything else is allowed to move? The answer requires you to understand that a knot is not defined by how it appears in a drawing—it is defined by whether there exists any sequence of allowed moves that can untangle it. This distinction between appearance and structure is the whole point.

Important Detail

A sketch on a page loses the over-under information at crossings. You need that data because changing which strand passes above or below another can create or destroy a knot without altering the pen marks themselves. The drawing only tells the full story when the crossing layers are recorded. When you draw a knot, the crossing looks identical whether the top strand goes over or under—the two lines just touch in the same place. But these two configurations are different topologically. Paying attention to which strand sits above requires you to add extra marks (usually a small gap) that show the underneath strand being interrupted.

Principle Behind It

A loop in space carries topological charge—information that motion cannot erase without violating the no-passing rule. The invariant is the property that stays constant across all legal reshuffling; it measures whether two curves are fundamentally different in structure. An invariant is a number, polynomial, or abstract property that you can compute from a knot, and if two knots have different invariants, they cannot be the same knot.

Failure Mode

Visual tangledness is not the knot. A knot diagram is meaningful only when crossings record which strand passes over and which strand passes under. Without that record, the drawing has lost the spatial question. Legal motion must preserve the embedded loop, so an untangling that passes strands through each other has changed the problem.

Course Role

Knots sharpen the idea of allowed moves. The same discipline later governs deformation of submanifolds, intersection counts, and paths in spaces of possible states. They make clear why passing through is not an innocent shortcut: it changes the relation being studied, just as crossing a forbidden state would change a configuration-space problem.
