Winding and linking

Winding and linking exist because going around something can be a stable relationship. A loop around a post can stretch and wiggle, but it cannot stop going around the post unless it crosses the post or breaks.

Concept Essay

Pick up a rubber band and slip it around a pen. Now pull the rubber band in all directions—it stretches, it bends sideways, it wraps oddly, but it never stops going around the pen. You could shape it into a perfect circle, a square, a figure-eight, or a wild tangle, and yet one fact remains: it still goes around the pen. This is winding. It is the observation that certain geometric relationships survive every shape change you make, as long as you follow one rule: nothing is allowed to pass through anything else. The rubber band does not know how round it looks. It only knows whether it encloses the pen or does not.

The deeper insight is that winding is not about how wide or how round the loop is, and it is not even about the pen itself, really. It is about a relationship between two things: the path and the obstacle. Imagine a rope looped once around a lamppost in a park. Tighten the rope, loosen it, tie knots in it, spiral it through the air, drape it in loose coils—the rope is still going around the post exactly once. The geometry of the loop has changed completely. Its length, its width, the number of twists in the fiber—all of these are different now. Yet the relationship of going-around-once has not budged an inch. This is the bedrock of winding: relationships are harder to destroy than shapes are. A shape can be stretched and deformed without limit, but a relationship persists. That persistence is what makes the relationship worth counting.

Now take two physical chains and link them together like links in a chain, not in a line but woven through each other. Even if you straighten each chain into a taut line, twist them into helices, bend them into spirals, or fold them into accordion shapes, you cannot separate them without breaking one of them in half or passing one through the other. They stay linked because there is a topological obstruction—a geometric fact about how their paths weave through space that no reshaping can erase. This is what linking means, and it extends the same idea from one loop and one obstacle to two loops interacting with each other. The chains do not care about their exact geometry. They only know that they are intertwined.

Why does direction matter? Suppose you wind a rope around a post going clockwise as you look down from above, then someone else winds another identical rope around the same post going counterclockwise, each one loop. If you overlay these two windings on the same post, they cancel each other out—the net effect is as if neither loop is really winding at all. But the opposite winds must both be counted: one positive, one negative. The sign tells the story. This is why winding numbers are signed integers, not just plain counts. Opposite directions truly destroy each other's effect, like a debt of five dollars paid off by a payment of five dollars, or a step forward followed by a step backward in the same direction. The mathematics must track this cancellation because the physics and geometry demand it.

The physical proof that these relationships survive is constraint-checking, and it reveals why topology is not just a word. Draw a loop in the plane around a point. Can you slide this loop off without it crossing the point? If the loop winds once around that point, then no—any attempt to move the loop away will require it to cross the point at some moment during the motion. The same logic holds for linking: draw two tangled loops. Can you wiggle and reshape them until they separate without either one passing through the other? If they are truly linked, the answer is no. The geometric fact that prevents it is that their curves, when traced through the space between them, weave in a way that no continuous motion can unweave. The obstruction comes from the global shape of the paths, not from any local detail.

Winding and linking train your eye to see that some relationships are topological—they belong to the shape of how things go around each other, not to the measured distances or the exact visual form at any one moment. A loop might look messy or elegant, short or stretched, thin or thick, but these details do not affect whether it winds around something. What counts is the route: does it go around, or does it not? How many times? In which direction? These questions stay meaningful even when all the visual details change radically. This retraining of intuition is why the concept appears in a course on topology and geometry—it teaches that rigid measurements like size, width, and exact position are far less stable than topological relationships like going around, linking, and tangling.

Beyond the immediate demonstration, winding and linking lay the groundwork for deeper ideas in topology. They prepare you for intersection numbers, which are counts of how many times one curve crosses another when you draw both in a plane, and for vector field indices, which measure how a direction field swirls around a point. In all these cases, the same thinking habit applies: attach a sign or orientation to each event (a crossing, a turn, a wind), count the signed total, and recognize that this signed count is an invariant—a number that survives all reasonable changes to the picture. Winding and linking are the simplest and most concrete examples of a shift in mathematical thinking: from measuring things like length and area to counting geometric relationships that persist. This is the foundation upon which the rest of the course builds.

First Principles

Take a loop of rope and place it around a post, then try to remove it without lifting the loop. You cannot—the loop has trapped a geometric relationship. Winding counts how many times a closed curve goes around an obstacle, and this count survives reshaping, stretching, or bending. Linking extends the idea to two loops: if they are tangled, no continuous motion through space can untangle them without one passing through the other. The question topology asks is not whether shapes look the same, but whether certain relationships can persist through every allowed deformation.

Important Detail

Direction and sign make all the difference. A loop winding clockwise around a post is opposite evidence from a loop winding counterclockwise, because opposite winds can cancel each other out. The count is signed, meaning positive and negative windings do not add together neutrally—they offset. Without tracking direction, you lose the most important information about what has actually been preserved.

Principle Behind It

The winding number is an integer attached to a closed loop relative to a fixed obstacle, and it measures the net number of counterclockwise turns the loop makes around that obstacle when you traverse it. Linking number counts crossings between two loops in a diagram, assigning positive and negative signs based on the orientation at each crossing; two loops are linked if this signed count is nonzero, meaning no smooth deformation in three-dimensional space can separate them.
