Method Families

Method families explain how the course turns pictures into reasons. They are the reusable proof moves beneath the lectures.

Replace a difficult object by an easier one only after the allowed motion, fixed data, and protected question have been named clearly enough for the reader to audit every step. The everyday problem is that a simpler picture is tempting, but it can lie if the route to it cuts, jumps, moves an endpoint, crosses forbidden material, or changes the fact being tested.

Deformation arguments

The human problem is that the original picture is often too tangled to reason about directly, but an uncontrolled simplification can answer the wrong question. The family asks how to change the picture while keeping the exact data the problem cares about: endpoints, boundaries, gluing, forbidden crossings, side choices, or the count being protected.

Build a number, sign total, or parity check that ignores harmless local changes but keeps the obstruction that every legal version of the picture must still obey. The everyday problem is that raw counts change whenever the drawing is cleaned up, so the course has to design a count where fake changes cancel and the forced information remains visible. The count earns trust only after that cancellation is explained.

Surviving-count arguments

The human problem is that a drawing can change while the underlying obstruction remains. A count gives the reader something stable enough to compare before and after the change, but only if it was designed for the changes the problem permits. The family asks which part of the changing picture deserves memory.

Connect small patches, boundary behavior, holes, handles, side choices, and gluing rules to conclusions that belong to the completed surface rather than one local drawing. The everyday problem is that each patch may look ordinary by itself, while the whole surface still forces a count, side reversal, boundary term, or defect that appears only after all pieces are joined. The full surface is the evidence carrier.

Surface bookkeeping

The human problem is that surfaces look simple nearby but can behave differently as wholes. This family asks how patches, boundaries, holes, handles, gluing rules, and orientation combine into whole-surface facts that local inspection alone misses completely. It teaches why a small ordinary-looking neighborhood is not the whole story.

Open
Ask whether required connections, loops, or strands can live in the chosen surrounding space without forbidden crossings, lost over-under data, or illegal pass-through moves. The everyday problem is not whether a drawing looks crowded, but whether the surrounding space gives enough legal room for every required route while preserving the data the problem says cannot change. The room rule decides the claim.

Drawing and embedding arguments

The human problem is to know whether one object can sit inside another without forbidden collisions. A drawing may suggest an answer, but this family asks for a reason that covers every drawing and every legal attempt. It replaces drawing frustration with an audit of the object, the room, and the forbidden moves.

Open
Turn mechanical or physical questions into questions about complete states, legal paths, forbidden regions, fixed points, equilibria, and barriers in the space of all possibilities. The everyday problem is that watching one motion can hide the real constraint, while the state space can show blocked routes, forced special states, or missing escapes before exact equations are solved. The model must record every needed freedom.

Motion through possible states

The human problem is to understand motion when exact equations or trajectories are unavailable. This family asks what the shape of possible states can force before anything is solved second by second or computed point by point. It lets the course reason about behavior by first making the space of possible behavior honest.
