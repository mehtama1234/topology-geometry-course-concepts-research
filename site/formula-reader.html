Formula Reader

The formulas in this course are compressed sentences about what is counted, what motion is allowed, why the count survives, and what conclusion the protected count can force.

How To Read A Formula Here

Start by asking what object the formula is talking about: a surface, a pair of meeting objects, a map, a vector field, or a space of possible states. Then ask what operation produced the numbers: splitting into pieces, assigning signs, walking around a defect, or comparing a graph with a diagonal. Finally ask why the number is allowed to speak after the picture changes. That last step is the mathematics.

A formula is useful only when it guards the right information. If the surface was modeled incorrectly, if the signs have no orientation, if the boundary was ignored, or if the defect is not isolated, the symbols may still be written down while the reason has failed.

Euler characteristic

chi = vertices - edges + faces

Plain reading: Take a surface that has been divided into pieces. Count corner points, subtract edge pieces, then add face pieces.

Why it survives: When a face is split by a new edge, both the edge count and face count change. The alternating account absorbs that artificial choice.

What it can force: The final number belongs to the surface type, not to one chosen drawing or mesh.

Reader check: Before using the number, ask what surface is being counted and whether boundaries or different kinds of cells have been accounted for.

Open concept
Signed intersection number

total = plus meetings - minus meetings

Plain reading: Count each clean meeting between two objects, but record whether the meeting agrees or disagrees with the direction rule of the surrounding space.

Why it survives: A legal motion can create two new meetings at once. In the ordinary case they have opposite signs, so the signed total does not change.

What it can force: If the protected total is nonzero, the objects cannot be pulled apart by legal motion.

Reader check: Before trusting the signs, ask where orientation comes from and whether the meetings are clean enough to count.

Open concept
Parity

only even or odd is kept

Plain reading: Forget the exact number and keep only whether the number is even or odd.

Why it survives: Some legal changes add or remove events two at a time. That can change the exact count while preserving oddness or evenness.

What it can force: An odd protected parity can prove that zero is impossible, even when the exact count is unknown.

Reader check: Use parity only when the allowed moves really do change the count by pairs.

Open concept
Fixed point as graph meets diagonal

fixed point means graph(f) meets diagonal

Plain reading: Turn a rule into a shape by drawing all input-output pairs. The diagonal is the set where input and output agree.

Why it survives: The equation f(x) = x becomes a meeting question. Earlier intersection reasoning can then decide whether that meeting can be avoided.

What it can force: A fixed-point theorem can prove that some point stays put without computing which point it is.

Reader check: Check the domain, boundary, and continuity of the rule before applying the theorem.

Open concept
Brouwer fixed point

every continuous self-map of a closed ball has a fixed point

Plain reading: Move every point of a filled ball somewhere inside the same filled ball, with nearby points still moving to nearby outputs.

Why it survives: The filled shape and its boundary block a continuous escape plan in which every point avoids itself.

What it can force: Shape can force existence without a formula for the answer.

Reader check: The closed ball matters. Changing the space can change the claim.

Open concept
Vector-field index

index = signed turning around a defect

Plain reading: Walk around a small loop enclosing a place where the arrow field vanishes. Watch how the nearby arrows turn during that walk.

Why it survives: The local arrow pattern can be moved or redrawn, but its signed turning survives allowed cleanup as long as the defect is treated honestly.

What it can force: A local failure of motion becomes countable evidence.

Reader check: Make sure the defect is isolated before assigning a single local index.

Open concept
Poincare-Hopf

sum of indices = Euler characteristic

Plain reading: Add the signed index of every isolated vector-field defect on a surface.

Why it survives: Defects may move, split, or cancel in controlled pairs, but the total has to match the surface's Euler characteristic.

What it can force: The surface controls the total failure of any arrow field on it.

Reader check: Do not read one equilibrium alone as the theorem. The statement is about the whole surface total.

The Common Pattern

Every formula above turns a flexible situation into a disciplined account. Euler characteristic forgets the chosen mesh and remembers the surface. Intersection number forgets temporary crossing pairs and remembers unavoidable meeting. Brouwer turns a rule into forced self-agreement. Poincare-Hopf turns local arrow failures into a whole-surface demand.

That is the first-principles point: mathematics is not adding symbols to a picture. It is choosing an account that survives the legal changes in the problem and is strong enough to rule something in or out.
