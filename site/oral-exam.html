Oral Exam

This page is the final no-notes readiness check. Each prompt should be answerable out loud in plain language, without starting from theorem names or hiding behind formal terms.

How To Use The Oral Exam

Answer each prompt before opening the related concept pages. Begin with the object being studied, then state the permitted move or comparison, the protected fact, and the conclusion that follows. If the answer starts with a term and cannot unpack it, return to the term translator or repair clinic.

The follow-up question is the stress test. It asks whether the reader can defend the explanation when a common shortcut is removed: a missing caption, a false model, an unstated theorem assumption, or a visual phrase that needs a proof contract.

Use a slow spoken order. First say what one thing in the problem represents. Next say what may change without changing the question. Then say what fact stays put while the change happens. Only after those three parts are clear should a theorem, count, source, or formal word enter the answer.

If the answer needs a picture, the picture must be treated as evidence. Point to the part of the picture that carries the object, the part that shows the allowed move, and the part that would break if the move were illegal. If those parts cannot be named, the picture is not yet doing mathematical work.

Readiness prompt

Explain the course in one chain

Prompt: Explain the whole course as one chain, starting from a paper strip and ending with constraints on motion, without listing topic names as a substitute for reasons. Make each step answer why the next idea is needed, not merely what the next lecture is called. The response has to sound like a person explaining why the idea is needed, not like a list of course labels. This keeps the question focused on first principles: object, allowed move, protected fact, conclusion, and limit.

Strong answer: A complete response says the course begins by showing that small local views can miss whole-shape facts. The paper strip is not a throwaway surprise; it makes the reader ask what survives a full trip around the object. From there the course turns surprise into method: legal deformation lets a hard picture be replaced by an easier one, quotient rules build spaces from gluing instructions, protected counts turn change into evidence, signed intersections make meetings auditable, fixed points turn self-agreement into a forced meeting, vector-field index records local arrow failure, and configuration spaces let shape constrain motion. The chain has to sound like one growing habit: name the object, state the legal move, protect the fact that survives, and then say what the protected fact forces. The strongest version connects the course reading rule to another field without pretending that the topology word automatically applies there. The answer stays plain by explaining why the detail matters before using the course name for it.

Must include: The answer must include a concrete early object, the allowed move that turns pictures into proof, one protected fact that travels through the course, and a later conclusion about forced meeting, forced defect, or blocked motion. The answer also needs one sentence saying what stronger claim remains unavailable after the reasoning is finished. The examiner can reject an answer that gives the topic name but not the everyday check behind it.

Common failure: A shallow response lists topics in order without saying why each topic is needed by the next one. It sounds organized but does not show how a paper-strip lesson becomes a method for proving later constraints. The fix is to slow down at the first unsupported leap and replace it with an object, rule, evidence, and limit. The corrected answer has to weaken the claim or restore the missing condition before moving on.

Follow-up: Where does the course first move from a visual surprise to a reusable proof method, what new obligation appears at that moment, and how does that obligation reappear in a late motion example? Name the early object and the later state-space or vector-field object. A good response answers by rebuilding the chain, not by repeating the first answer with different topic names. Use the linked concepts, topology-vs-geometry, deformation, configuration-space, only after the answer has already made their work visible.

Defend a deformation argument

Prompt: Given a messy drawing, explain when simplifying it is a proof and when it changes the problem by breaking the allowed move. Name the original object, the legal motion, the protected fact, and the first shortcut that would change the question. The answer needs one concrete failure case, because the boundary of the method often teaches the idea better than a smooth example. This keeps the question focused on first principles: object, allowed move, protected fact, conclusion, and limit.

Strong answer: A complete response treats simplification as valid only when every step is an allowed move for the original question. The easier endpoint is not evidence by itself; the legal route to it is the evidence. Name the starting object, the rulebook for motion, and the feature that must not change: endpoint order, obstacle avoidance, boundary behavior, gluing rule, side choice, crossing data, or state restriction. Also name a tempting illegal shortcut, such as cutting a loop, passing through a forbidden obstacle, moving a fixed endpoint, or changing the state space while pretending the same problem is still being solved. The response avoids broad slogans by naming the actual evidence that survives the allowed change. The answer stays plain by explaining why the detail matters before using the course name for it.

Must include: The answer must name one allowed motion, one forbidden shortcut, the fact that survives the legal motion, and the exact moment where the shortcut would change the problem. It must also say why the cleaned picture still has authority over the original picture. This requirement keeps the oral answer aligned with the course reading rule of checking before naming. The examiner can reject an answer that gives the topic name but not the everyday check behind it.

Common failure: A shallow response says the drawing can be cleaned up because it looks equivalent, without naming the rule that makes the cleanup legal. It treats visual resemblance as proof and loses the contract that made deformation useful. A repair starts by asking which object the answer is about and which condition keeps the conclusion attached to that object. The corrected answer has to weaken the claim or restore the missing condition before moving on.

Follow-up: What feature would make the simplified drawing solve a different problem, how would you catch that change before trusting the picture, and what would the corrected proof have to protect instead? State the check in everyday words before using any formal name. The answer has to name the exact point where a tempting shortcut stops being legal. Readiness prompt

Turn a count into evidence

Prompt: Explain why the course trusts some counts but rejects raw visible counts, using a local change that appears during deformation. Start with a count the eye can see, then explain what legal motion threatens it and what repaired account survives. Start from the object in the problem, then move through the allowed change, the protected evidence, and the limit of the conclusion. This keeps the question focused on first principles: object, allowed move, protected fact, conclusion, and limit.

Strong answer: A complete response treats a count as evidence only after it is tested against the legal moves. Raw crossings, cells, regions, or defects may appear and disappear during harmless redrawings, so the first visible number may remember only the drawing. The protected count is designed to survive: signs can make opposite events cancel, parity can ignore paired changes, Euler characteristic can survive subdivision, and index can record the turning around isolated defects while local patterns move. Explain a local threat to the count and then show why the repaired account still speaks about the original problem. It shows why the detail matters: removing that detail would make the conclusion unsupported or change the problem being answered. The answer stays plain by explaining why the detail matters before using the course name for it.

Must include: The answer must include a local event that threatens a count, the cancellation or whole-surface rule that protects the count being used, and the conclusion the surviving account is allowed to force. It must separate visible events from protected evidence. Include the failure boundary in plain words: what one change, missing condition, or overread source would break the claim. The examiner can reject an answer that gives the topic name but not the everyday check behind it.

Common failure: A shallow response says numbers are invariants because the course uses them, reversing the reason. The course uses them because their survival has been checked against the moves the problem allows. The mistake is treating a number as evidence before auditing its behavior. The failure tells the learner exactly what to repair before the answer becomes trustworthy. The corrected answer has to weaken the claim or restore the missing condition before moving on.

Follow-up: What changes during a legal move, what part of the count refuses to change, why does that surviving part become evidence, and what claim would be too strong for that count? Give one example where the visible count changes but the protected account does not. It must say what survives the change and what conclusion becomes weaker, stronger, or unavailable. Use the linked concepts, invariant, intersection-number, euler-characteristic, only after the answer has already made their work visible.

Readiness prompt

Read a theorem contract

Prompt: Explain how to use a theorem name without letting the name replace the argument, including the assumptions that must be checked first. Build the response like a contract: object, allowed comparison, protected evidence, conclusion, and limit. The response has to sound like a person explaining why the idea is needed, not like a list of course labels. This keeps the question focused on first principles: object, allowed move, protected fact, conclusion, and limit.

Strong answer: A complete response starts with the theorem's contract. It names the object, checks the assumptions, states the allowed move or comparison, identifies the protected evidence, and gives only the conclusion the theorem can actually force. For Brouwer, the object is a continuous rule from a filled ball back into itself, and the conclusion is that at least one point returns to itself, not where it is. For Poincare-Hopf, the object is a vector field with isolated defects on a surface, and the conclusion is a total index constraint tied to Euler characteristic, not a solved motion path. The theorem name belongs after the setup is clear enough that the reader can see why the theorem is allowed to speak. The strongest version connects the course reading rule to another field without pretending that the topology word automatically applies there. The answer stays plain by explaining why the detail matters before using the course name for it.

Must include: The answer must say what the theorem needs before it speaks, what exact conclusion it gives, what stronger claim remains unproved, and which condition would break the use. It must show that the theorem is being applied to the right object. The answer also needs one sentence saying what stronger claim remains unavailable after the reasoning is finished. The examiner can reject an answer that gives the topic name but not the everyday check behind it.

Common failure: A shallow response invokes Brouwer, Poincare-Hopf, or Euler characteristic as authority before naming the object, assumptions, and conclusion limit. It makes the theorem sound like permission to skip the proof contract. The reader hears a name but cannot test the claim. The fix is to slow down at the first unsupported leap and replace it with an object, rule, evidence, and limit. The corrected answer has to weaken the claim or restore the missing condition before moving on.

Follow-up: What would make this theorem application invalid even if the theorem itself is true, which assumption would fail first, and how would you weaken the conclusion to stay honest? Say what stronger result remains unavailable after the repair. A good response answers by rebuilding the chain, not by repeating the first answer with different topic names. Model a physical motion problem

Prompt: Explain how the course turns a physical motion problem into topology without overclaiming, starting from what a single state records. Include the allowed paths, the forbidden states, the physical meaning of a blockage, and the limit of the topological conclusion. The prompt is meant to expose whether the course-wide idea has become usable reasoning rather than remembered vocabulary. This keeps the question focused on first principles: object, allowed move, protected fact, conclusion, and limit.

Strong answer: A complete response translates the physical story into a space of possible states before topology acts on it. One point must represent one complete state, with all positions, angles, contacts, boundary conditions, or choices the question cares about. Forbidden states must be removed, real freedoms must not be forgotten, and a real motion must become a continuous path through allowed states. Only then can a hole, wall, disconnected region, fixed point, or index constraint in the state space say something about the original system. State the limit too: a correct topological model may prove a blockage or forced state without describing every second of motion. A complete answer names the object first and then lets the concept name shorten an explanation that already exists. The answer stays plain by explaining why the detail matters before using the course name for it.

Must include: The answer must include what one state records, what is forbidden, which route or obstruction question replaces the original motion story, and what conclusion would overclaim beyond the model. It must say what physical detail topology leaves for later methods. Each item has to be tied to the same object so the answer does not quietly change problems halfway through. The examiner can reject an answer that gives the topic name but not the everyday check behind it.

Common failure: A shallow response draws the physical object and says topology applies, but never defines the state space that topology is supposed to inspect. It leaves the theorem with no honest object to see. The mistake is confusing a machine drawing with the space of possible states. It also fails to transfer, because another field cannot borrow the idea without knowing what condition made it valid. The corrected answer has to weaken the claim or restore the missing condition before moving on.

Follow-up: Which false freedom or missing obstacle would make the model lie about the original problem, where would it enter the state space, and what physical conclusion would become unreliable? Explain whether the error invents a route, erases a route, or changes the obstruction. The follow-up is designed to force a second pass through the reasoning, using a changed condition or a new object. Readiness prompt

Separate fixed points and equilibria

Prompt: Explain the difference between a fixed point and an equilibrium in plain language, naming the rule or field behind each claim. Start from the object being studied, then say what special state is forced and what evidence detects it. The examiner is listening for a chain of reasons: what is present, what may change, what cannot change, and what follows. This keeps the question focused on first principles: object, allowed move, protected fact, conclusion, and limit.

Strong answer: A complete response says a fixed point belongs to a rule that sends points back into the same space, while an equilibrium belongs to an arrow field or motion law where the arrow vanishes. For a fixed point, the evidence is self-agreement: the input and output are the same point, and the graph can be compared with the diagonal. For an equilibrium, the evidence is local arrow behavior: the arrow at the point vanishes, and nearby arrows may turn in a way that gives an index. Both can be forced special states, but the proof must name the object before using the shared intuition that something is staying put. The answer is strong when every formal word can be replaced by an everyday sentence that still explains the same work. The answer stays plain by explaining why the detail matters before using the course name for it.

Must include: The answer must include self-agreement for a map, vanishing arrows for a vector field, the evidence each object uses, and the danger of mixing the two objects. It must say which conclusion belongs to which carrier. A term name alone does not count as inclusion unless the answer says what job that term performs in the argument. The examiner can reject an answer that gives the topic name but not the everyday check behind it.

Common failure: A shallow response says both are places where nothing moves, which hides the rule or field that gives the statement meaning. It uses the feeling of stillness instead of the mathematical carrier. The answer then cannot tell which theorem or index evidence applies. The examiner can expose the weakness by changing one condition and asking whether the same sentence still works. The corrected answer has to weaken the claim or restore the missing condition before moving on.

Follow-up: Which object are you studying: a map from points to points, or arrows attached to points, what evidence belongs to that object, and what would be false if you used the other language? State the wrong conclusion that the mixed wording would invite. This makes the oral exam a check on understanding, because the learner has to repair the idea while speaking. Use the linked concepts, fixed-points, equilibrium, vector-field-index, only after the answer has already made their work visible.

The Passing Standard

A passing answer is not long. It is accountable. It names the thing under study, says what may change, says what survives, and names what the surviving evidence can force. It also says what the explanation does not prove.

An answer fails when it hides the hard part inside a familiar word. Saying invariant, fixed point, index, quotient, or configuration space is not enough. The reader has to say what is being compared, what rule makes the comparison legal, and why the surviving fact matters for the problem that started the lecture.

The best answers also know where to stop. A count may force parity without locating every crossing. A fixed-point theorem may force existence without giving a recipe for finding the point. A source may support a mathematical family without proving every sentence in a course explanation. Naming those limits is part of understanding.

Use this page after the lecture reconstruction drills and before claiming readiness. If one prompt feels easy only because the answer repeats the page wording, close the page and rebuild the idea from a different lecture example. Readiness means the explanation can move to a new object while keeping the same allowed move and protected fact.
