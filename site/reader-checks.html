Reader Checks

Use these checks when a page feels easy for the wrong reason. Each one turns a common confusion into a concrete question about legal moves, protected evidence, whole-shape structure, or modeling.

How To Use This Page

Do not memorize these as warnings. Use them as a reading routine. When a proof moves a picture, ask what motion is legal. When a proof counts something, ask why the count survives. When a proof uses signs, ask where the signs come from. When a physical example appears, ask what mathematical space represents the possible states.

The goal is to make each page harder to misunderstand. A first-principles explanation should let the reader audit the move, not only remember the conclusion.

Reader check

The drawing is being treated as the object

What goes wrong: A square with edge labels, a graph of a map, or a configuration space is a code for relationships. The ink is not the final object.

Why it matters: The same visible drawing can describe different spaces when the edge rule changes, so the rule has to be read before the picture can be trusted.

Ask instead: What rule does this picture represent, and what relationships must survive if I redraw it?

Reader check

The allowed motion is not stated

What goes wrong: A deformation can only prove something after the legal moves are named. Cutting, crossing through, moving endpoints, or dropping a boundary can change the problem.

Why it matters: The whole proof rests on the promise that the motion preserves the question. Without that promise, a simpler picture may solve a different problem.

Ask instead: Which motion is legal here, and which feature is being protected while the picture moves?

Reader check

A raw count is trusted too early

What goes wrong: Visible crossings, cells, or defects may change under harmless redrawings. The useful count is the one designed to survive those changes.

Why it matters: Euler characteristic, intersection number, parity, and index all work because fake changes cancel. The raw number usually does not have that protection.

Ask instead: What local change can happen, and does the proposed count stay fixed when it happens?

Reader check

Local evidence is mistaken for global evidence

What goes wrong: Every small patch of a Mobius strip looks ordinary. Every small patch of a sphere can carry an arrow. The whole object may still refuse a consistent choice.

Why it matters: Topology often begins exactly where local inspection stops. The obstruction may appear only after a full trip, a full sum, or a full gluing.

Ask instead: Can the local choice be carried around the whole object without contradiction?

Reader check

Signs are used without checking orientation

What goes wrong: Plus and minus signs must come from a direction rule. Without orientation or a local sign convention, signed arithmetic may not be defined.

Why it matters: A sign is not a decoration attached to a count. It records how objects meet or how arrows turn inside a setting where direction has meaning.

Ask instead: What gives the sign its meaning in this space?

Reader check

A failed drawing is treated as impossibility

What goes wrong: One bad attempt does not prove no legal drawing exists. A topological obstruction has to defeat every legal attempt.

Why it matters: This is the difference between drawing skill and proof. The proof must name a protected fact that no redraw can remove.

Ask instead: What fact survives all attempts, and why does it block the desired drawing?

Reader check

The exceptional case is studied before the ordinary case

What goes wrong: Tangencies, triple meetings, and non-isolated defects can hide the stable mechanism. The clean case should be understood first.

Why it matters: Ordinary clean cases allow isolated crossings, signs, and indices to be assigned. The exceptional moment is then used to explain how ordinary cases change.

Ask instead: What happens after a tiny legal nudge, and what changes only at the singular moment?

Reader check

The theorem is pasted onto the wrong model

What goes wrong: Applications need an honest state space, rule, boundary, and protected quantity. If the model has the wrong freedoms or barriers, the conclusion may describe the model but not the situation.

Why it matters: The topological theorem only sees the model. If the model omits a real motion or adds a false wall, the conclusion can be mathematically correct and physically irrelevant.

Ask instead: Does the mathematical space contain exactly the states and forbidden moves in the original problem?

Reader check

A fixed point is expected to be computed

What goes wrong: Fixed-point theorems often prove existence without naming the point. That is not a weakness when the goal is to prove that escape is impossible.

Why it matters: The course repeatedly values forced existence. Knowing that a point, meeting, or defect must exist can be the central answer even when its exact location is unavailable.

Ask instead: Is the page proving where the point is, or proving that some point must exist?

Reader check

A formula is read without its protected account

What goes wrong: Symbols such as chi, index, or f(x) = x are easy to repeat while missing what they count. The formula is the end of a reasoning contract, not the beginning.

Why it matters: The course's equations work only because a legal motion, sign rule, boundary condition, or surface account has already been named.

Ask instead: What is being counted, why does that account survive, and what conclusion can it force?

Reader check

A term is remembered without its job

What goes wrong: Names such as manifold, quotient, index, and invariant matter only when they explain what can move, what is counted, or what is forced.

Why it matters: A name should compress a working idea, not replace it. If the action behind the word is unclear, the reader has vocabulary without understanding.

Ask instead: What work does this word perform in the argument?
