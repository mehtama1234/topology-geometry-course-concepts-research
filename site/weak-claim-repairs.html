Weak Claim Repairs

This page turns shallow course sentences into checkable explanations. Each repair keeps the language plain, but makes the object, permission, protected detail, and useful limit visible.

How To Use This Page

Start with a sentence that sounds smooth but could be hiding the hard part. Do not ask whether the sentence uses a correct word. Ask what the reader can inspect after reading it. A strong sentence lets the reader name the object, say what is allowed to change, point to the detail that survives, and say what conclusion that detail can support.

The repair should usually be one or two plain sentences, not a new technical paragraph. It should replace an empty phrase with a testable claim. If the original says the pictures are the same, the repair says which rule survived. If it says the theorem applies, the repair names the theorem's object and conclusion. If it says the source supports the page, the repair names the exact kind of support.

Weak claim repair

Same shape means same answer

Weak claim: These two pictures are the same topologically, so the answer is the same for the original problem without naming which moves preserved the object.

Why weak: The sentence skips the object and the permission. A reader cannot tell whether the gluing rule, endpoint data, boundary order, or forbidden crossing rule survived the move. Same under what rule is the whole question; without that rule, the phrase same topologically can hide a changed problem.

First-principles repair: Say what the object is first. Then say the picture was changed only by moves that keep the gluing rule, boundary data, and forbidden regions fixed, so the question being answered is still the original question. The repaired sentence should make the legal route visible before the simplified endpoint is trusted.

Detail to check: Check one local move at a time and ask whether it changes the rule that defines the object, not whether the final drawing looks cleaner. If one step moves fixed data, crosses forbidden matter, or changes an identification rule, the final picture no longer speaks for the starting one.

Where to use: Use this repair on deformation, quotient, knot, route, and surface pages before saying that a simplified drawing proves the original claim.

Weak claim repair

The count is invariant

Weak claim: This number is invariant, so it proves the obstruction and shows the desired motion cannot happen without checking the allowed local changes.

Why weak: The word invariant does all the work without naming the allowed changes or explaining why the number survives them. A reader can repeat the label while still not knowing why this count is allowed to travel from one drawing to another.

First-principles repair: Name the moves the problem allows, then explain what happens to the count under each move. If new pieces appear in matched pairs, or if signed contributions cancel, say that plainly before using the number as evidence. The repair should also say what conclusion the surviving number is strong enough to force.

Detail to check: Ask what event could change the raw count and what rule prevents that event from changing the protected count. If the answer cannot describe one threatening local event, the invariant claim is probably only a name.

Where to use: Use this repair on Euler characteristic, parity, winding, linking, intersection-number, and graph-planarity explanations whenever a number is used as proof.

Weak claim repair

The theorem applies

Weak claim: By the theorem, the required point or obstruction exists, so the example has the conclusion we wanted before the theorem's contract is checked.

Why weak: The sentence asks the theorem to speak before proving that the current object is the kind of object the theorem is about. It also hides the limit of the theorem's conclusion, so existence can slide into construction or a total count can slide into a solved motion path.

First-principles repair: Before the theorem name, list the object, the rule or field on it, the needed condition, and the exact conclusion type. Then say whether the theorem gives existence, equality, parity, obstruction, or a count. The repaired sentence should make the theorem sound narrower but more trustworthy.

Detail to check: Check that the rule maps the space back into itself, the field includes all defects, or the surface/count is the one named by the theorem. Then check the conclusion limit: what does the theorem not locate, compute, or construct?

Where to use: Use this repair on fixed-point, Brouwer, Poincare-Hopf, vector-field index, and theorem-contract pages whenever a theorem name appears before its object and conditions.

Weak claim repair

The picture makes it clear

Weak claim: The picture makes the conclusion obvious once the reader sees the drawing in the right way, even though the proof feature has not been named.

Why weak: A picture can suggest the right idea while still hiding the rule that makes the idea true. The reader needs to know what part of the picture is evidence and what part is only a helpful drawing. Without that distinction, a diagram becomes persuasion instead of proof.

First-principles repair: Point to the exact feature being used: a side change, a forced crossing, a signed pair, a boundary order, a hole in a state space, or a whole-surface total. Then explain why that feature survives legal changes. The repair should say what the reader should watch if the picture is redrawn.

Detail to check: Ask whether redrawing the picture, changing scale, or moving a curve legally would keep the named feature. If the feature disappears under a harmless redraw, it was probably a drawing artifact rather than mathematical evidence.

Where to use: Use this repair on lecture pages where a demonstration carries the explanation from a concrete object into a mathematical claim.

Weak claim repair

The model captures the motion

Weak claim: The configuration space shows whether the system can move from one physical position to another before one state and the forbidden states are named.

Why weak: The sentence skips the meaning of one point in the model and does not say which physical states were removed as impossible. It treats the diagram as if topology can inspect motion before the model has earned its connection to the physical system.

First-principles repair: Say what one point of the configuration space records: every position, angle, choice, or relation needed for one allowed state. Then name the collision, boundary, or blocked states that have been removed. The repaired sentence should also state what kind of conclusion the model can support: a route exists, a route is blocked, or a special state is forced.

Detail to check: Trace one physical motion and one path in the state space, and confirm that every intermediate point represents a legal physical state. Also check the reverse: every modeled path used in the proof should translate back to a motion the system could actually make.

Where to use: Use this repair on configuration-space, product-space, motion-family, and transfer-lab pages whenever a physical story is turned into a path question.

Weak claim repair

Signs cancel

Weak claim: The plus and minus terms cancel, so only the total matters for the final answer without explaining the shared sign convention.

Why weak: The sentence treats signs as if they were automatic labels. It does not explain where the shared direction rule comes from or why opposite signs are comparable. A plus and a minus can cancel only after the page earns a common sign convention.

First-principles repair: Name the orientation rule, boundary direction, or local direction comparison that assigns signs. Then explain the local pair creation or cancellation event that keeps the signed total stable. The repaired sentence should make cancellation a checked event, not an arithmetic wish.

Detail to check: Ask whether a plus sign in one place and a minus sign in another place were assigned by the same rulebook. If the rule cannot be carried across the object, the signed total may not be defined.

Where to use: Use this repair on boundary orientation, intersection number, vector-field index, and formula-reader pages whenever cancellation is used as evidence.

Weak claim repair

Local behavior determines the whole surface

Weak claim: The local pieces add up to the global conclusion, so the whole surface must obey the formula even before the complete account is checked.

Why weak: The sentence says local and global without explaining the bookkeeping bridge between small neighborhoods and the whole object. It names the direction of the argument but not the mechanism that carries local facts into a surface-wide conclusion.

First-principles repair: Describe one local contribution first, such as a turn, defect index, angle shortage, or cell piece. Then explain the rule for adding all contributions across the whole surface and what total they must match. The repaired sentence should say why no isolated patch is enough by itself.

Detail to check: Ask whether every local contribution is included exactly once and whether the surface used for the total is the same surface being studied. Missing a boundary term, hidden defect, or glued edge can make the global conclusion belong to the wrong object.

Where to use: Use this repair on Gauss-Bonnet, Poincare-Hopf, Euler characteristic, triangulation, and local-to-global theme pages when local evidence is summed.

Weak claim repair

The source supports this

Weak claim: The listed source supports the explanation on this page and gives the reader enough authority without naming the exact supported claim.

Why weak: The sentence does not say what kind of support is being claimed: exact lecture wording, recovered caption evidence, course-arc inference, background theorem, or broader source family. A citation becomes shallow when it hides that difference.

First-principles repair: Name the sentence being supported and the source role behind it. If the source supports only a background family or a formal theorem, weaken the page sentence so it does not pretend to be exact lecture evidence. The repaired sentence should say what the source can carry and what remains interpretation.

Detail to check: Ask what stronger sentence would no longer be supported if the caption stayed missing or the source were only a background reference. If the answer is unclear, the support claim needs a narrower label.

Weak claim repair

This is just an example of the concept

Weak claim: This example illustrates the concept and helps the reader see why the idea is useful without saying what the example lets them inspect.

Why weak: The sentence does not say what the example lets the reader inspect. A useful example should expose the object, the allowed move, the protected fact, or the failure condition. If it only says the example is useful, the reader still does not know what to look at.

First-principles repair: Say what the example makes visible. It may show one point in a state space, one legal deformation, one crossing pair, one glued boundary, one defect, or one source boundary. Then say what the reader should test. The repaired sentence should turn the example into an inspection task.

Detail to check: Ask whether the example would still teach the idea if the formal concept name were removed from the paragraph. If not, the example is probably leaning on the name instead of showing the job.

Where to use: Use this repair on concept pages, theme pages, subtheme pages, and lecture examples that currently rely too much on naming.

Weak claim repair

The big picture is about shape

Weak claim: The big picture is that topology studies shape and ignores geometry when solving these problems, regardless of which measured details still matter.

Why weak: The sentence is too broad and can mislead the reader into thinking size and angle never matter. The course is more precise: it chooses which facts should survive a chosen class of changes. Geometry and topology are levels of evidence, not slogans in a contest.

First-principles repair: Say the course studies questions where the important fact survives bending, redrawing, cutting into valid cells, or moving through allowed states. Geometry is not thrown away; it is kept when the question needs it and set aside when it distracts from the surviving fact. The repaired sentence should name the protected fact, not only the word shape.

Detail to check: Ask what the current question protects: side information, boundary order, a count, a path obstruction, a self-agreement point, or a whole-surface total. If that protected fact is not named, the big-picture sentence is too broad to teach.

Where to use: Use this repair on course-synthesis, math-why, topology-vs-geometry, themes, and first-viewport overview pages where summary language can overreach.

The Repair Standard

A repair passes only when the reader can test it without trusting the writer's tone. The repaired sentence should answer four questions: what thing is being studied, what operation or comparison is being made, what fact is protected, and what would make the sentence false.

A repair fails when it simply swaps one formal word for another. Replacing same with homeomorphic, number with invariant, rule with map, or source with reference does not improve the explanation unless the sentence also says what those words are doing in the current problem.

The strongest repairs also know where to stop. A theorem may force existence without finding the point. A count may prove obstruction without drawing every impossible motion. A source may support a mathematical family without proving exact lecture wording. Stating that limit is not weakness; it is part of the explanation.

Page Review Method

Use this page as a pass over the whole companion. On each lecture, concept, theme, subtheme, method-family, theorem, and source page, find the sentence that carries the most weight. If that sentence does not name its object, permission, protected detail, or limit, repair it before adding more material.

The goal is not to make every page longer. The goal is to make the decisive sentences less slippery. A short page can be strong if its main claim is checkable. A long page can still be shallow if it leans on words like obvious, invariant, theorem, source, or example without saying what the reader can inspect.

For a lecture page, attach the repair to the concrete demonstration. For a concept page, attach it to the example that shows the concept's job. For a paper page, attach it to the exact source role. For a theorem page, attach it to the assumption and conclusion contract.

When the repair is done, read the old sentence and the new sentence aloud. The new one should make one extra obligation visible, not merely sound more polished.

Connection To The Bigger Goal

The companion is supposed to reach a reader who does not already know the formal language. That does not mean removing mathematics. It means showing why the mathematical sentence earns its authority. The reader should see why a twist survives flattening, why a count survives redrawing, why a theorem has permission to speak, and why a model is faithful to the original motion problem.

This page is a standing audit against reusable empty writing. If a sentence could appear unchanged in any other course, it probably needs a repair. The repaired sentence should belong to this course because it names this course's object, move, evidence, and failure condition.
