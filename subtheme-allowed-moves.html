Allowed moves

Allowed moves define the exact question being asked. Before saying two shapes are the same, before simplifying a drawing, and before claiming a count survives, the course must state what may move, what must stay fixed, and what kind of accident is forbidden.

Subtheme Essay

Allowed moves are the rulebook behind every deformation argument in the course. Before deciding that two pictures are equivalent, the reader has to know what is permitted: stretching, sliding, bending, dividing a surface, attaching a piece, crossing through, moving endpoints, or preserving boundaries. The reader also has to know what is not permitted. Endpoints may have to stay on named boundary points. A gluing rule may have to stay attached. Two paths may be forbidden to cross. A collision state may have been removed from the model. Changing any one of those rules changes the problem. A knot is only knotted because passing through itself is forbidden; a disk path puzzle is only obstructed because paths may not cross.

From first principles, an allowed move defines sameness. Two objects are not the same or different in the abstract until the problem names the motion being allowed. They are the same under a stated kind of motion. A loop may be the same after bending but not after breaking. A surface may be the same after stretching but not after changing an edge identification. A vector field may be the same after cleanup only if the index evidence survives.

This subtheme is the first thing to inspect when a proof feels too easy. If the simplification used an illegal move, the argument may have solved a different problem. Tokieda's pictures work because they make the allowed motion visible enough for the reader to audit. The proof has to show how the picture got simpler, not merely present the simpler picture.

Allowed moves connect early and late material. They govern Mobius-strip experiments, disk paths, handle movement, intersection counting, knot untangling, fixed-point graphs, and vector-field defects. In the paper-strip examples the legal move may be bending without tearing. In the disk puzzle it may be drawing paths without crossing. In a vector-field proof it may be changing arrows while keeping defects isolated except at controlled moments. Each topic has its own rulebook, but the reader's habit is the same: name the legal motion before trusting the conclusion.

The plain rule is that every claim of sameness needs a phrase after it: same under what changes? Once that phrase is known, the rest of the proof can be judged. Without it, the page may sound clear while quietly changing the mathematical question.

First Principles

A rule changes the object the proof is about. If a knot can be cut, it can be untied. If paths can pass through each other, a crossing obstruction disappears. If endpoints, boundary order, gluing instructions, or forbidden collisions are allowed to drift, the original problem has been replaced by another one.

Related Concepts

Concept

Generic position

Generic position exists because special coincidences are too brittle to reason from. If three things meet at exactly one point, or a curve just kisses another curve, a tiny nudge can change the picture. The course wants arguments that survive tiny nudges, so it first moves the picture away from accidental perfection.

Open
Concept

Deformation

Deformation exists because exact drawings often hide the answer. If stretching a curve does not change the question, then a complicated curve can be replaced by a simpler one. The purpose is not to make a cleaner-looking picture; it is to preserve the right fact while throwing away distracting measurement.

Open
Concept

Knots and links

Knots and links exist in the course because a curve in space can remember how it is embedded. Length, roundness, and exact position can change, yet the loop may still be unable to become a plain circle by legal motion.

Open
Concept

Surgery

Surgery exists because classification needs controlled operations, not passive looking. By removing a known piece and gluing in another, the course can expose handles, crosscaps, and boundaries and can compare surfaces through repeatable moves that a reader can audit.
