Singular moments

A special accident can be where the action happens: two intersections are born, two cancel, a tangent contact appears, or a defect ceases to be isolated for a moment during motion. The problem is to understand the accident without letting it make the whole argument brittle.

Subtheme Essay

A singular moment is an exceptional event between ordinary pictures. Two intersections are born, two cancel, a tangency appears, or a vector-field defect stops being isolated. The course does not ignore these moments. It uses them to understand how stable pictures change without changing the protected total.

From first principles, a singular moment is a transition point. Just before it, the picture is ordinary. Just after it, the picture is ordinary again. At the moment itself, something fragile happens: a touch, a merge, a split, or a non-isolated failure. The proof studies the nearby ordinary pictures to see what the transition is allowed to change.

This subtheme keeps generic position honest. The ordinary case is studied first, but singular moments explain transitions between ordinary cases. Pair creation and cancellation are not nuisances; they reveal how the invariant survives. The exception is studied just long enough to prove that the count changes in a controlled way.

Singular moments often occur during motion, so the audit question is what they are allowed to change. If the event creates opposite signs or preserves parity, it cannot destroy the invariant the proof is using. If it changes the protected total, then the supposed invariant was not protected after all.

The reader's check is to compare before and after. What local events appeared or disappeared? Did they come in a pair? Did their signs cancel? Did a defect split without changing total index? The singular instant is the audit point for the proof: it tells the reader exactly how two ordinary pictures remain part of one legal story.

Related Concepts

Concept

Generic position

Generic position exists because special coincidences are too brittle to reason from. If three things meet at exactly one point, or a curve just kisses another curve, a tiny nudge can change the picture. The course wants arguments that survive tiny nudges, so it first moves the picture away from accidental perfection.
