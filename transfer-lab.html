Transfer Lab

This page checks whether a reader can move a course idea into a fresh situation without hiding behind formal words. Each case starts with an everyday scene and asks for the object, allowed move, protected fact, wrong transfer, and course bridge.

How To Use A Transfer Case

Do not begin with a theorem name. Begin by naming what the mathematical object is in the new situation. Then state what changes are allowed, what fact is protected while those changes happen, and which course idea this matches.

The wrong-transfer line matters because beginners often transfer the visible story instead of the mathematical job. A useful comparison does not say only that two examples look alike; it says what object, move, and protected fact are the same kind of thing.

Transfer case

Rubber band around a mug handle

Situation: A rubber band is looped around the handle of a mug. You can stretch the band and slide it on the mug, but you cannot cut it or pass it through the ceramic.

Object: The object is the loop together with the mug handle acting as an obstacle. The exact roundness of the band is not the issue. What matters is the relationship between the closed loop and the hole through the handle.

Allowed move: The allowed move is a continuous sliding and stretching of the loop while the band stays uncut and never crosses through the handle. The band may get longer, thinner, or uglier; it may not pass through ceramic or open itself.

Protected fact: The protected fact is whether the loop is linked around the handle. That relation survives many changes of shape because the only moves that would remove it are exactly the moves the problem forbids.

Course bridge: This transfers the knot-and-link habit from the course: do not trust the drawing's neatness; ask what relationship cannot be removed by legal deformation. The mug handle plays the role of forbidden matter, and the band plays the role of a loop whose route memory matters.

Wrong transfer: A shallow response says the band looks circular or messy. That talks about geometry of the drawing, not the topological relation that blocks removal. It also misses the illegal move: pulling the band through the handle or cutting it open.

Reader task: Explain whether the band can be freed without cutting it, name the exact move that would be illegal, and say which relationship survives every legal stretch.

Transfer case

Two walking routes around a fountain

Situation: Two routes across a plaza start and end at the same gates, but one route goes around a fenced fountain and the other does not.

Object: The object is not the pavement drawing alone. It is the plaza with the fenced region removed and the two paths inside the remaining space. The fence changes the space because it creates a place the path is not allowed to cross.

Allowed move: The allowed move is to slide a route through open walking space while keeping its endpoints at the gates and never crossing the fence. The path may wiggle, shorten, or lengthen, but the endpoints and forbidden region define the same route problem.

Protected fact: The protected fact is whether the route winds around the missing region. A fenced hole can block one path from being changed into the other because any attempted slide would have to cross the fence or move an endpoint.

Course bridge: This is the same first-principles move as loops on surfaces: the missing region matters because it changes which deformations are legal. The course habit is to read the obstacle as part of the object, not as decoration in the drawing.

Wrong transfer: A shallow response says both paths are just lines between the same gates. That ignores the obstacle that makes the state space different and treats endpoint agreement as enough by itself.

Reader task: Decide whether one route can be deformed into the other while respecting the fence, say what the fence contributes to the proof, and name the illegal shortcut that would make the routes look equivalent.

Transfer case

Matching arrows on a weather map

Situation: A small weather map shows wind arrows around a calm point. Nearby arrows turn as you walk around the calm point.

Object: The object is the arrow field near an isolated calm point, together with a small loop around that point. The calm point is not just a dot; it is a place where the arrow field fails, and the surrounding arrows are the evidence.

Allowed move: The allowed move is to clean or redraw nearby arrows without removing the calm point from the loop or dragging another calm point inside. The loop must continue to isolate the same failure so the turning account is about one defect.

Protected fact: The protected fact is the total turning of the arrows during one walk around the loop. That turning can survive redrawings even when individual arrows are sketched differently.

Course bridge: This transfers vector-field index from the course: local arrow behavior can become signed evidence without solving the motion everywhere. The everyday map becomes mathematical only after the reader walks around the defect and reads the surrounding arrows.

Wrong transfer: A shallow response tries to predict the full weather. The course idea only needs the local turning account around the defect. It also must not count every calm-looking point unless the point is isolated and has a readable surrounding pattern.

Reader task: Walk around the calm point in words, say what has to stay isolated before an index-like count is meaningful, and state what this local count does not predict.

Transfer case

Moving a sofa through a hallway

Situation: A sofa must move from one room to another through a hallway without hitting the walls. The sofa can rotate and slide, but cannot pass through walls.

Object: The object is the space of all allowed sofa positions and rotations, not just one drawing of the hallway. One point in that space must record enough information to know where the sofa is and how it is turned.

Allowed move: The allowed move is a continuous path through allowed states where every intermediate position avoids collision. The sofa cannot jump through a tight corner, and the model cannot forget rotation if rotation is part of the physical motion.

Protected fact: The protected fact is whether the start state and end state lie in the same connected region of allowed states. If a wall or narrow turn separates those regions, no continuous legal motion connects the two poses.

Course bridge: This transfers configuration-space modeling: a motion problem becomes a route problem in the space of possible states. The course idea is not to stare harder at the hallway, but to build the right state space and inspect its allowed paths.

Wrong transfer: A shallow response stares only at the floor plan. The proof needs the larger space that records both location and angle. It also has to remove collision states before claiming a route exists.

Reader task: Name what one point of the state space represents, what would count as a forbidden state, and how a path in that space translates back into real sofa motion.

Transfer case

Stirring coffee in a cup

Situation: A spoon stirs coffee inside a round cup, and after the motion every bit of coffee has moved somewhere in the same cup.

Object: The object is the filled cup as a disk-like region and the rule sending each starting point to its ending point. The rule, not the stirring story by itself, is what fixed-point thinking inspects.

Allowed move: The allowed move is a continuous self-map of the filled region, with no point sent outside the cup. Nearby starting points must end nearby, and the final positions must still live inside the same filled region.

Protected fact: The protected fact is that a continuous self-map of the right kind of filled region cannot avoid some self-agreement. The conclusion is about at least one starting point matching its ending point, not about all points returning.

Course bridge: This transfers fixed-point thinking: the question is not where the point is, but why some point must return to itself. The everyday motion becomes course mathematics only after the domain, rule, and self-map condition are named.

Wrong transfer: A shallow response says every point returns or that the theorem computes the point. The safe conclusion is existence of at least one fixed point, and only after the self-map contract has been checked.

Reader task: State the domain, the rule, and the exact conclusion without pretending to locate the fixed point or claiming that every bit of coffee returns.

Transfer case

Drawing a map on a folded sheet

Situation: A map is drawn on a sheet whose opposite edges are taped together according to arrows. After taping, some apparent edges are no longer edges.

Object: The object is the finished surface created by the taping rule, not the flat sheet before the rule is applied. The arrows and tape are instructions for sameness, so the completed object may have fewer boundaries than the drawing suggests.

Allowed move: The allowed move is to reason after the edge identifications have been made, treating glued points as the same point. Redrawing the sheet is harmless only if the same identification rule is still being used.

Protected fact: The protected fact is the route and boundary behavior of the completed surface after the taped edges are treated as one. A path that looks like it hits an edge on paper may continue through the glued edge in the finished surface.

Course bridge: This transfers quotient-space reading: the drawing is a code, and the gluing rule tells the reader what space the code describes. The course habit is to read the rule before trusting the picture.

Wrong transfer: A shallow response counts the border of the flat sheet as a real boundary after the taping rule has already removed it. That reasons about the code instead of the finished object.

Reader task: Say which points become the same point, what boundary claim changes after the identification, and which feature of the flat drawing should no longer be read literally.

Transfer case

Counting dents on a squeezed ball

Situation: A soft ball is squeezed, dented, and redrawn with different patches, but it is never punctured or glued to another piece.

Object: The object is the surface of the ball under legal deformation, not the number of visible dents in one picture. The surface remains the carrier even when its drawing, patching, or local wrinkles change.

Allowed move: The allowed move is to bend and redraw the surface without tearing, puncturing, or changing how the surface is connected. Changing the cell account is allowed only when it still describes the same surface.

Protected fact: The protected fact is a whole-surface count such as Euler characteristic, not the raw count of dents or patch lines. Dents may appear or disappear; the useful count is the one designed to survive legal changes to the surface account.

Course bridge: This transfers the stable-count habit: choose the count designed to survive the legal changes, not the count easiest to see. The everyday squeezed ball becomes a test for separating drawing noise from whole-surface evidence.

Wrong transfer: A shallow response counts every wrinkle as mathematical evidence. Wrinkles are drawing noise unless the argument says why they survive. The repair is to ask which count remains the same after the surface is redrawn honestly.

Reader task: Identify one visible count that is not protected, one whole-surface fact that is protected, and the illegal change that would stop the protected fact from applying.

Transfer case

A robot arm avoiding a blocked zone

Situation: A simple arm has two joints and must move from one pose to another while its tip avoids a blocked zone on the table.

Object: The object is the space of joint choices after forbidden choices have been removed. One point records one complete arm pose, including enough joint data to know where the tip is and whether the pose is allowed.

Allowed move: The allowed move is a continuous path through allowed joint choices, with no intermediate pose entering the blocked zone. The arm may move gradually through joint space, but the path cannot pass through a forbidden pose just because the drawing on the table looks convenient.

Protected fact: The protected fact is whether the allowed-pose space has a route from the start pose to the target pose. A blocked zone can create a hole, wall, or disconnected region in the space of choices.

Course bridge: This transfers late-course application thinking: topology studies the shape of possible states before detailed motion equations are solved. The course does not need every control input before it can ask whether the allowed state space has a route.

Wrong transfer: A shallow response draws only the arm in physical space. The topological question lives in the space of all allowed joint choices, after forbidden poses have been removed and real freedoms have been kept.

Reader task: Describe one complete state of the arm, explain what removed states do to the route question, and say what conclusion would overclaim beyond the state-space model.

The Transfer Test

A transfer is strong when the reader can replace the everyday story with a course object and still keep the allowed move and protected fact intact. If the answer changes only the words while leaving the object unspecified, the transfer has not happened yet.

A complete transfer answer has three sentences. First, it says what one point, loop, field, surface, or route represents. Second, it says which change is legal and which change would break the problem. Third, it says what fact survives that legal change and what conclusion that survival can support.

If those sentences cannot be written in everyday language, return to the matching concept page before using the case as evidence of understanding.
